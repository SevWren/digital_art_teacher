 Creating an Alien Character for a Sci-Fi Short Film with Blender & Substance 3D

 # Character Art # Interviews # Blender # ZBrush # Substance 3D Painter

 Artur Ledur talked to us about character development for a sci-fi short film under a one-week deadline and discussed working on the design and armor, texturing, and matching the character to the film's scenes.

 Introduction

 Hi everyone! My name is Artur Ledur, and I'm a 3D Character Artist working across games and film. I currently work at the VFX studio FornoFX, while also freelancing as a character artist for game and film studios around the world. I've always been passionate about art and entertainment, but pursuing a career in this industry once felt like a distant dream for a young Brazilian artist. Because of that, I spent several years working with art direction and design for advertising. A few years ago, I decided to finally pursue that old dream and began studying 3D character art while building my portfolio. Fortunately, less than a year after making that career transition, I had the opportunity to work on my first game project as a Character Artist for 171, an AA Brazilian open-world third-person shooter developed by Betagames Group, with a realistic art style. That experience opened the door to many other opportunities, and since then I've had the privilege of contributing to several projects, including two animated feature films, a sci-fi live-action short film, AA game titles with Hermit Crab Game Studio, and a sci-fi live-action TV series, alongside various projects involving games, collectibles, VFX, and independent developers from around the world. My main tools today are ZBrush, Maya, Blender, Substance 3D Painter, Marmoset Toolbag, and UE5, but I see them as just part of the process. The most interesting part is learning how to choose the right workflow for each project and each artistic direction.

 Today, I'm driven by the opportunity to create memorable characters and contribute to immersive narratives that resonate with audiences. I want to keep growing as an artist and be part of projects that push the industry forward and create meaningful experiences for people.

 Getting Started

 The film's director, Pedro de Lima Marques, was one of my professors at university. Besides teaching, Pedro also runs FornoFX, a VFX and post-production studio. I was fortunate enough to present my work to him at the right time, when he was looking for a 3D Artist to join the team working on two animated feature films they were developing. I joined the team, and a few months later, Pedro brought a new project into the studio's scope: GenZ vs Aliens, a sci-fi live-action short film. He then gave me the amazing opportunity to take full responsibility for the visual development of the film's main antagonist, the Alien.

 GenZ vs Aliens is a sci-fi short film about four teenagers who disconnect from social media for a weekend at their grandparents' house, only to find themselves caught in an alien invasion that involves the abduction of cows and local residents. Forced to reconnect with the digital world they were trying to escape, they must use social media to fight back and possibly save the world. Directed by Pedro de Lima Marques and produced by Bactéria Filmes and RS Fantástico, the film combines humor, visual ambition, and social commentary around technology and generational differences.

 Inspiration & Technical Task

 The character design was a major challenge because we only had preliminary concept art that didn't fully translate the visual direction we wanted for the Alien. So, there were several challenges to solve, and for each one, I tried to find a design solution that would work within the context of the film.

 The first challenge was bringing the character into a realistic visual language that could convincingly exist alongside real actors. I wanted to achieve this through a combination of realistic features, especially in the surface treatment, exploring things like dirt, wear, and material variation across both the organic skin and the armor.

 Another challenge was making it clear, even in most of the shots and close-ups, that this was not a human. I pushed the proportions further by giving the character longer legs, very large hands with three fingers, and a larger overall body proportion compared to a human. I also designed the armor as an exoskeleton, leaving several areas of the organic body exposed and creating a stronger connection between the creature and the technology.

 For one of the backlit shots, where the Alien appears mostly as a silhouette, I wanted to make its shape immediately feel more alien. The solution was to add an extra pair of arms, which gave the character a much more distinctive silhouette. I also wanted to push the character towards a more horrific and unsettling look. Since the Alien wears a helmet, we needed a way to make it clear that there was an alien underneath, especially in a close-up shot of the head. I made the visor transparent, allowing part of the alien face to be visible, and added membrane-like organic sacs emerging from the sides of the helmet, creating a more grotesque appearance.

 The one-week deadline was another important factor in the process, so I had to make design decisions with production time in mind. I wanted to quickly establish the main forms and move towards a final design while leaving most of the tertiary details for the texturing stage rather than spending time sculpting them into the high-poly.

 Overall, what I really wanted to explore with this project was the combination of an organic creature and hard-surface technology, and how these two visual languages could work together as part of the same character. It was a great opportunity to take the initial concept further while designing specifically for the needs of the film.

 Modeling

 For the initial research, I gathered a large collection of visual references in PureRef, focusing primarily on creature design and hard-surface armor from films and games. ArtStation and Pinterest were my main sources for references during this stage.

 I then started the blockout in ZBrush, using exploratory sculpting to find the right visual language for the character. At this stage, I was constantly combining elements from the references with the original concept, while keeping the requirements of the film in mind.

 Once the main proportions and shapes were established with the blockout mesh, I got approval to start refining the character. Normally, I would spend much more time on the blockout to get closer to the final look before moving forward. However, because of the very tight deadline, I had to solve several design and form decisions directly on the final mesh.

 For the metal armor, I used a technique that allowed me to preview a clean result while keeping the process non-destructive. I started with a plane using Dynamic Subdivision in ZBrush, with specific thickness, bevel, and crease configurations, and then extruded it along the sculpted blockout. This allowed me to explore the armor while maintaining clean topology and a good preview of the final forms, without having to commit to a destructive workflow.

 Because of the tight schedule, I also decided to leave most of the tertiary details for the texturing stage instead of sculpting everything into the high-poly. This helped keep the modeling process more agile while still allowing me to achieve the level of detail required for the final shots.

 Retopology & Unwrapping

 For the metal armor topology, as I mentioned earlier, I modeled it directly in ZBrush using the non-destructive technique I described. Once all the pieces were in the position and shape I wanted, I applied the modifiers and exported a mid-poly version of the armor to Blender for UV unwrapping. Since the topology was already clean and production-ready, I didn't need to retopologize the armor, which saved me a significant amount of time.

 For the body, I used Blender's retopology tools, with a workflow similar to Quad Draw in Maya. I focused on maintaining proper edge loops and clean polygon distribution, particularly around areas that would need to deform properly during animation. Once the topology was completed, I unwrapped all the meshes in Blender using UDIMs and multiple UV tiles. Since the character was intended for a pre-rendered production rather than a real-time game, I had more freedom to use higher texture resolution and a higher polygon count.

 Texturing

 For baking and texturing, I always use Adobe Substance 3D Painter.

 After baking, I usually start with a first color pass using solid colors to establish an approximate overall look. From there, I build the main materials. For the exoskeleton, I wanted a combination of metal, carbon fiber, and rubber, with several emissive elements to create a mix of military and alien technology. I also added a lot of tertiary detail directly during the texturing stage. I used a collection of mechanical alphas from Keos Masons to create small bas-relief details, symbols, gaps, and mechanical shapes across the armor. Since the deadline was so tight, doing these details in texturing rather than sculpting them into the high-poly saved a significant amount of time while still giving the armor a lot of visual complexity.

 Once the main materials were established, I started working on the wear and dirt. This was probably the stage where the character really started to feel alive to me. Details such as roughness variation, dirt, blood stains, scratches, dented edges, and signs of use and contact can tell a lot about a character and suggest what they have been through, even beyond what is explicitly shown on screen. These small imperfections helped make the armor feel more believable, grounded, and connected to the character's story.

 For the alien skin, several elements were also developed during the texturing stage. The blue organic sacs on the abdomen and arms are a good example. I wasn't completely sure what the final look should be at first, but I found an interesting direction by combining emissive elements with organic detailing. This helped push the creature further towards a more organic and alien look, while keeping those characteristics visible even in the areas covered by the armor. I also added markings on the face and hands, almost like a form of alien war paint, to give the character another layer of backstory.

 Lighting

 For the two scenes in the film, we used Image-Based Lighting (IBL), capturing HDRIs and on-set reference data at both locations where the Alien appears. We also captured chrome and matte balls to record the lighting and material response of the environments. I then brought these references into Blender and used them for light matching, recreating the lighting conditions of each scene as accurately as possible. This helped integrate the CG character with the live-action footage and ensure that the materials and lighting responded naturally to the environments.

 For the presentation renders, I posed the character in ZBrush and then sent it back to Blender, where I used a basic studio lighting setup to showcase the character and materials. To make the presentation more visually appealing, I also created an energy orb using Blender's particle system, giving the Alien a more mystical and otherworldly feel. The blue organic sacs on the abdomen and arms were established as the source of the Alien's power, connecting these organic elements to the generation and summoning of the energy orbs.

 Conclusion

 The biggest challenge was definitely the one-week deadline. It added another layer to the challenge, but also brought extra energy to the creative process. I had to be very intentional about where to spend my time, prioritizing the elements that would have the biggest impact on the final result. This also taught me how important it is to understand the character beyond the asset itself. Knowing where the character exists within the film, how it will be presented to the audience, what the camera needs to see, and what role it plays in the story can directly influence the creative and technical decisions throughout the process. Instead of trying to make everything perfect at every stage, I focused on establishing the main forms and visual language quickly, refining what was most important for the shots, and leaving secondary details for later stages of the pipeline.

 For beginners, I think references are one of the most valuable tools you can have. In professional projects, we often work from a well-defined 2D concept and our job is to translate that vision into 3D, which is an extremely important skill to develop. But when you have the opportunity to contribute to the visual development of a character, references become even more important. No matter how creative we are, building a strong visual library and constantly studying real-world shapes, materials, creatures, costumes, and other artists' work gives us a much stronger foundation to create from.

 I'd also like to give a special thanks to the entire FornoFX team, especially the film's director, Pedro de Lima Marques, for the incredible opportunity and for trusting me with the Alien's design. I'd also like to thank Alex Moreira, the mocap supervisor and animator, whose work was essential in bringing the character to life and achieving the final result we see in the film.

 Ultimately, I think creativity is not just about having ideas, but also about knowing how to find the right references, understand the context, and make intentional decisions with the time and resources you have. That mindset was probably one of the most valuable takeaways from this project, and I hope some of these insights can be useful in your own journey as well!

 Artur Ledur, 3D Character Artist
