### 1. Traditional Consumer B&W Line Art Pipeline
This pipeline applies to personal, non-commissioned pen-and-ink or similar physical line work (sketchbooks, personal illustrations, hobbyist pieces). Emphasis is on learning, enjoyment, and iterative exploration rather than external approval or reproduction standards.

1. Research and ideation: Gather references and define subject, mood, and approximate composition.
2. Thumbnails: Produce multiple small, rapid compositional sketches.
3. Subject studies and rough construction: Explore forms, proportions, and gesture on scrap or sketch paper.
4. Light pencil underdrawing: Transfer or redraw the preferred composition lightly on final paper (graphite or non-photo blue).
5. Inking: Apply permanent black lines with dip pens, fineliners, or brushes, working from main contours to secondary details and textures. Line weight is adjusted by hand pressure and tool choice.
6. Optional tonal work: Add hatching, solid blacks, or limited grey media if desired.
7. Cleanup and finishing: Erase residual pencil, correct minor errors with white media or scraping, and store or photograph the physical original.

### 2. Traditional Commercial B&W Line Art Pipeline
This pipeline applies to client-commissioned or published illustration work executed with physical media. Additional gates exist for approvals, technical specifications (size, resolution for scanning/reproduction), and archival quality.

1. Brief and research: Receive client brief; gather references; confirm size, reproduction method, deadline, and style requirements.
2. Thumbnails and concept sketches: Present multiple compositional options for client selection.
3. Refined pencil underdrawing: Produce a clean, detailed construction drawing at final size on high-quality paper, often using non-photo blue for easy dropout after scanning.
4. Client review of pencils (common gate).
5. Inking: Execute final line work with professional tools (G-pens, mapping pens, brushes, India ink). Line hierarchy, weight variation, and cleanliness are prioritised for reproduction. Assistants may handle secondary elements in larger studios.
6. Tonal treatment if required: Solid blacks, hatching, or limited screentone-style applications.
7. Final cleanup, scanning (typically 600 dpi or higher monochrome), digital touch-up if needed, and delivery of both physical original and digital files meeting client specifications.
8. Revisions and final approval.

Deadlines, multiple approval rounds, and technical print constraints distinguish this from consumer practice.

### 3. Traditional Consumer B&W Manga Line Art Pipeline
This pipeline describes personal or doujinshi-style manga production using primarily physical tools. Flexibility is high; formal editorial review is absent or self-imposed.

1. Concept and outline: Develop premise, characters, and rough plot.
2. Character designs: Create informal reference sketches.
3. Script or notes: Outline dialogue and page flow.
4. Nēmu / thumbnails: Rough page layouts focusing on panel arrangement, pacing, and balloon placement.
5. Pencils: Detailed underdrawing on manga manuscript paper (or ordinary paper) at preferred size, often in blue or light graphite.
6. Inking: Final black lines with G-pens, maru-pens, or fineliners. Panel borders ruled; backgrounds may be simplified.
7. Screentones or manual toning: Apply adhesive screentone sheets or hand-drawn hatching/solids.
8. Lettering: Hand-letter balloons and sound effects or leave space for later digital text.
9. Cleanup, scanning if desired, and personal finishing or self-printing.

### 4. Traditional Commercial B&W Manga Line Art Pipeline
This is the classic Japanese industry pipeline for serialised or published work, still widely referenced even as digital tools proliferate. It is highly structured, editorially gated, and frequently involves assistants.

1. Concept development with editor: Premise, characters, and overall direction are refined collaboratively.
2. Character reference sheets: Formal turnarounds and expression studies for consistency.
3. Script: Page-by-page breakdown.
4. Nēmu (name/thumbnails): Rough storyboards submitted for editorial approval; primary revision stage.
5. Pencils: Full-size detailed underdrawing on B4 manuscript paper (or equivalent), commonly in non-photo blue. Assistants may begin backgrounds.
6. Inking: Lead artist inks key figures and critical lines; assistants often ink backgrounds, panel borders, and mechanical details using G-pens, mapping pens, etc.
7. Screentones: Application of adhesive tone sheets (or solid blacks) for value, texture, and mood. Assistants frequently handle extensive toning.
8. Lettering and sound effects: Integrated or prepared for typesetting.
9. Final cleanup, scanning (high-resolution monochrome), digital correction if required, and submission to publisher for printing (typically reduced to B5 magazine or smaller tankōbon size).

### 5. Established Consumer B&W Digital Line Art Pipeline
This is the standard personal digital workflow for pure black-and-white line art (illustration or simple comics) using software such as Clip Studio Paint, Photoshop, or Procreate. Flexibility and easy revision are prioritised.

1. Ideation and research: Collect digital references; define concept.
2. Thumbnails: Rapid digital sketches, often on a low-resolution or separate canvas. 
3. Rough sketch / construction: Loose underdrawing on a dedicated layer (or multiple layers for figures, backgrounds).
4. Clean line art: New layer (frequently vector) for refined black lines using pressure-sensitive brushes that emulate traditional pens. Line weight and hierarchy refined iteratively; vector tools allow easy correction.
5. Optional tonal work: Additional layers for solid blacks, hatching brushes, or digital greyscale/screentone patterns.
6. Cleanup and organisation: Adjust contrast, remove stray marks, organise layers, convert brightness to opacity if needed for transparency.
7. Export: Save working file and export final image (PNG, TIFF, etc.) at desired resolution.

Layers, undo history, and stabilisers permit extensive iteration that is impractical traditionally; many consumers skip formal thumbnails or merge stages.

### 6. Established Commercial B&W Digital Line Art Pipeline
This is the professional digital counterpart used for client illustration or published manga/comics. It incorporates technical specifications, approval gates, and consistency controls while leveraging digital advantages.

1. Brief and research: Confirm client or publisher requirements (final size, DPI—commonly 600+ for monochrome line art—file format, style).
2. Thumbnails / concepts: Digital sketches presented for approval.
3. Refined construction / pencils: Detailed underdrawing at final resolution on organised layers (or folders), often using non-destructive tools. Character reference sheets locked for consistency in sequential work.
4. Client or editorial review of construction stage.
5. Clean line art / inking: Dedicated line layers (vector preferred for scalability and editability). Professional brush settings emulate G-pen or mapping-pen behaviour. Assistants may receive separate files or layers for backgrounds.
6. Tonal treatment: Digital screentone layers, solid blacks, or greyscale with controlled patterns; layer masks and clipping used extensively.
7. Lettering (if applicable): Separate text layers or balloons placed according to reading order and safe areas.
8. Final technical cleanup: Verify resolution, colour mode (monochrome or greyscale), bleed/safe areas, layer organisation, and file naming. Proof at print size.
9. Delivery of layered working files plus flattened high-resolution finals; revisions handled non-destructively where possible.

Commercial digital pipelines emphasise file organisation, non-destructive editing, print-ready technical standards, and version control far more rigorously than consumer practice. Hybrid workflows (scan traditional pencils then ink digitally, or vice versa) are common.

**Summary of distinctions**  
Consumer pipelines favour flexibility, fewer gates, and personal preference. Commercial pipelines add formal approvals, technical print specifications, consistency mechanisms (especially character sheets), and often collaborative labour. Traditional methods rely on irreversible physical marks and physical media management; digital methods exploit layers, vectors, and unlimited undos while preserving analogous stage logic. Manga pipelines, whether traditional or digital, place greater weight on sequential storytelling, panel pacing, and the nēmu review stage than standalone line-art illustration.
