# PTM Review — Beginner Accessibility (Team 2C)

## Summary

Total issues found: **27**

| Check type | Count |
|---|---|

Several entries are clean and genuinely beginner-accessible — the Composition section is largely strong. The problems cluster in the Colour section (too many entries use colour theory vocabulary to explain colour theory vocabulary) and the Perspective section (some plain names still require their own explanation). There are also three significant duplicate clusters that need editorial attention.

### Hue — Check C

**Field:** Use in output
**Problem:** The use-in-output sentence is: "The colour's name — which colour it actually is — is called its hue. A fire engine and a brick wall are both red: they share a hue even though one is brighter and newer-looking than the other." This is written as a definition, not as something a teacher would naturally say inside a coaching response. It sounds like reading from a textbook. A beginner receiving this mid-coaching would feel they are being formally taught terminology rather than helped with their painting.
**Suggested fix:** "Your sky and your water are both blue — they share the same basic colour family even though one is much darker and duller than the other. Shifting one of them toward green-blue or violet-blue would give them their own identity without breaking the blue mood."

### Saturation / Chroma — Check A, Check C

**Field:** Plain name
**Problem:** "How vivid or washed-out a colour looks" is the plain definition, not the plain name. The plain name listed is "How vivid or washed-out a colour looks" — but that is a full sentence definition, not a name. A plain name should be short enough to use in a heading. Compare the good examples in the matrix: "Dark-and-light skeleton," "Object-versus-background pop." This entry needs a short name.
**Suggested fix for plain name:** "Colour vividness — how punchy or faded a colour looks"

**Field:** Use in output
**Problem:** "Think of it like a room where every single person is shouting at the same volume — no one stands out." This is good, but the sentence immediately before it uses no context for the beginner: "Right now, almost every part of your image is equally vivid." The word "vivid" hasn't been introduced to this person yet in the coaching context. This entry relies on the beginner already knowing the word.
**Suggested fix:** Replace "equally vivid" with "equally punchy and intense" and add a one-phrase anchor: "...equally punchy and intense — like a room where everyone is shouting at the same volume."

### Value (in colour context) — Check D

**Field:** Entire entry
**Problem:** This entry is a near-duplicate of the "Value" entry in the Light and Value section. Both explain the same concept (how light or dark something is, ignoring colour), use nearly identical black-and-white-photo examples, and have the same core advice. The only difference is a slight colour-context framing. A beginner receiving both in different parts of a coaching session would be confused about why the same idea is being re-explained under a new name. The two entries should be merged with a cross-reference, or the Colour section entry should be a single-line pointer: "See 'How light or dark something is' in the Light and Value section — the same idea applies here."
**Suggested fix:** Replace the duplicate "Value (in colour context)" entry with: "Value (in colour context) — *See 'How light or dark something is' in the Light and Value section. The same rule applies here: before adjusting colours, check the black-and-white version of your image.*"

### Extension / Area ratio — Check A, Check B

**Field:** Plain name
**Problem:** "Extension / Area ratio" is the technical name. The plain name is listed as "How much space a colour takes up compared to how vivid it is" — but again this is a sentence definition, not a name, and the phrase "how vivid it is" still uses the word "vivid" which has not been explained to this beginner. The plain name needs to be short and self-contained.
**Suggested fix for plain name:** "Small-but-punchy — why a tiny patch of a strong colour can outweigh a huge area of a quiet colour"

**Field:** Plain definition
**Problem:** "A small patch of an intense, vivid colour can visually balance a much larger area of a quiet, muted colour — so you do not need to spread vivid colours everywhere to make them feel present in an image." The words "intense," "vivid," and "muted" all appear without having been explained first. A beginner who doesn't yet understand what "vivid" means will not understand this definition.
**Suggested fix:** "A tiny splash of a strong, eye-catching colour can get more attention than a huge area of a soft, dull colour — so you do not need to cover the whole image in strong colours to make one area stand out."

### Display-referred vs scene-referred workflow — Check A, Check B

**Field:** Plain name
**Problem:** "Whether your file stores colours as they look on screen or as the original light values were captured" is a definition, not a name. More critically, the terms "display-referred" and "scene-referred" are professional pipeline vocabulary that even intermediate hobbyists will not know. The plain name does not rescue this.
**Problem (definition):** The plain definition includes "scene-referred means your file stores the raw light data before any screen adjustments have been applied — the distinction matters when colour precision is important for professional output." The phrase "raw light data" and "professional output" are still technical and vague. The definition also contains two sentences.
**Additional concern:** The use-in-output section begins with "If you are starting from a raw camera file as the base of your painting..." — this assumes the beginner knows what a raw camera file is. The entry also explicitly says "For most hobbyist digital painting, you are working in display-referred mode and do not need to think about this." That sentence is a strong signal this entry should be flagged as advanced/optional in the PTM itself, or removed from coaching use entirely.
**Suggested fix for plain name:** "Screen-ready vs raw — whether your file's colours are already set up for display or still need adjusting"
**Suggested fix for plain definition (one sentence):** "Most digital painting files are already set up to display correctly on screen; a raw camera file is not, and painting directly on top of one without adjusting it first can make your painted colours look wrong compared to the photo underneath."

### Local colour (palette restriction / dominant hue family) — Check A, Check D

**Field:** Plain name and entire entry
**Problem:** The plain name is "Choosing a limited set of colours and sticking to them." That is a description of an action, not a name. More importantly, this entry is already almost entirely covered by the "Palette (restricted palette)" entry later in the same section. Both entries address the concept of deliberately limiting the colour set before painting, use similar language, and offer similar use-in-output advice. The "Local colour (palette restriction / dominant hue family)" entry also collides with the earlier "Local colour" entry (which correctly defines local colour as "what colour an object actually is without coloured lighting"). The bracketed technical names in the heading are confusing — they are three different concepts bundled together under one entry.
**Suggested fix:** Split this entry: keep the genuine "local colour" definition where it is, then delete "Local colour (palette restriction / dominant hue family)" and absorb its content into the "Palette (restricted palette)" entry. The two distinct ideas — what local colour is vs how to restrict a palette — should not share a heading.

### Split complementary — Check C

**Field:** Use in output
**Problem:** "Your palette is currently built around [main colour] and its direct opposite, which is creating a lot of tension everywhere. One way to keep the energy but soften the clash is to shift slightly off the direct opposite..." This coaching sentence assumes the beginner already understands what "direct opposite" means in a colour context. Without having the colour wheel concept freshly in mind, "direct opposite" is meaningless. If this entry appears in a coaching response without the colour wheel entry being established first, the beginner will be lost.
**Suggested fix:** Add a brief anchor: "...built around [main colour] and its direct opposite on the colour circle — the colour sitting exactly across from it — which is creating a lot of tension everywhere. One way to soften the clash is to use the two colours that sit just beside that opposite instead of the opposite itself."

### Triadic — Check B

**Field:** Plain definition
**Problem:** "A colour arrangement that uses three colours placed at equal intervals around the colour circle — like the points of a triangle — producing a palette that feels lively and varied while still being balanced." This is two sentences. The spec requires one sentence only.
**Suggested fix:** "A three-colour arrangement where the colours are spread evenly around the colour circle — like points of a triangle — producing a palette that feels lively and balanced without being chaotic."

### Tetradic — Check B

**Field:** Plain definition
**Problem:** "A colour arrangement using four colours that form two pairs of opposites — like two complementary pairs placed at right angles to each other on the colour circle — giving a lot of variety but requiring careful management so no single colour dominates unintentionally." This is one sentence but it contains three layered ideas: (1) four colours, (2) two pairs of opposites, (3) careful management requirement. The phrase "placed at right angles to each other on the colour circle" is geometric language that most beginners will not visualise correctly. The definition doesn't actually explain what tetradic means in plain English — it explains it in colour wheel geometry.
**Suggested fix:** "A four-colour arrangement where the colours form two pairs of opposites — like picking two completely different pairs of opposite colours from the colour circle at once — which gives a rich, varied palette but easily becomes chaotic if no single colour is given more space than the others."

### Station point — Check A, Check B

**Field:** Plain name
**Problem:** "The viewer's position — where in space the person looking at the scene is standing." This is a plain-English description but it doesn't function well as a short, usable name. The word "station" has no obvious meaning to a beginner and the plain name doesn't anchor to anything physical or memorable.

### Cone of vision — Check A, Check B

**Field:** Plain name
**Problem:** "The comfortable viewing zone — the area of the scene that can be drawn without distortion." The word "distortion" in a plain name creates anxiety in a beginner without explaining what distortion looks like or why it matters. "Comfortable viewing zone" is okay but slightly corporate-sounding.
**Suggested fix for plain name:** "The safe drawing zone — how wide an angle you can draw before things start looking stretched or bent"

**Field:** Plain definition
**Problem:** "The roughly circular area of a scene that the eye can see without objects appearing stretched or bent unnaturally, which corresponds to looking within about 60 degrees of the central line of sight." The clause "which corresponds to looking within about 60 degrees of the central line of sight" is technical measurement language. A beginner has no reference for what 60 degrees of vision means. The 60-degree fact belongs in the example or use-in-output, not the plain definition.
**Suggested fix:** "The zone directly in front of the viewer where objects look natural and undistorted — anything you draw too far toward the edges of an unusually wide scene can start to look stretched or bent."

### Value family vs Light group vs Shadow group — Check D, Check B

**Field:** Entire entries (three entries)
**Problem:** These three entries use identical examples. All three share the same example text: "In a black-and-white photo, everything is either bright or dark. A good painting keeps all the bright things in one 'team' and all the dark things in another team. The moment those teams mix everywhere into the same muddy grey, the image looks flat and hard to read." Using the exact same example for three different entries is a significant problem for a beginner. When the assistant delivers all three concepts in a single coaching response, the beginner will hear the same example three times and wonder if the same thing was said three times or if there are actually three different things. The repetition erodes trust.

Additionally, "Light group" and "Shadow group" are two halves of the same concept. The "Value family" entry already covers both. Asking a beginner to track three separate named concepts that all mean "keep your darks together and your lights together" is more confusing than clarifying.
**Suggested fix:** Merge "Light group" and "Shadow group" into a single entry: "The bright team and the dark team." Give them distinct examples — the light group entry should use an example that illustrates why all lit surfaces need to stay in the same brightness range, while the shadow group entry should illustrate why shadows drift lighter and break up. The Value family entry can keep the current example and serve as the introduction; the merged light/shadow entry can be a deeper look.

### 6. Scale vs Proportion — overlapping concepts (minor)

- **"Proportion"** — Composition section, line 198. Plain name: "How the sizes of things relate to each other."
- **"Scale"** — Composition section, line 206. Plain name: "How big something is compared to its surroundings."

These are distinct enough to deserve separate entries, but the plain definitions and use-in-output examples overlap significantly.
