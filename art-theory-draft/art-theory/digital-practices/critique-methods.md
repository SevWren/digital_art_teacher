# Critique Methods

**Status:** Work in progress.

This file is about helping you check your own work — not about enabling the agent to evaluate it. All of the checks described here are things you perform on your own screen. The agent's role is limited to describing what it observes, mapping observations to known patterns, and identifying technical issues. Whether your image is good, finished, or worth continuing is your judgment as the artist.

## Observational Checks

These checks are performed by you. Run them in the order listed — they progress from coarsest (overall structure) to finest (individual edges and technical delivery).

### Squint test
Half-close your eyes until the image blurs. The detail disappears and only the largest light and dark patches remain visible. Check whether those patches alone make a clear, readable picture — a strong centre of attention, a sense of depth, a legible subject. If the overall pattern does not read when the detail is gone, the value structure needs attention before further rendering.

### Thumbnail test
Zoom out to 20–25% of your working size, or export the image and open it at small size. At this scale, proportional errors, crowded compositions, and imbalanced focal areas become visible that are hidden at full size. Verify the main subject is still clearly identifiable and the image reads as a coherent scene.

### Flip the canvas horizontally
Most software has a horizontal flip option (sometimes called "mirror canvas"). When you flip, the image is temporarily reversed left-to-right. The reversal removes your familiarity with the piece and reveals proportion errors, tilts, and balance problems that became invisible through repeated looking. Check proportions, the position of the focal area, and whether any part feels heavier or more crowded than intended. Flip back and correct what you noticed.

### Desaturate a flattened copy
Create a flattened copy of your image and remove all colour (usually via a Desaturation or Hue/Saturation adjustment, or by converting to grayscale). With no colour present, check whether form still reads as three-dimensional, whether the focal area has the highest contrast, and whether depth is still visible in the spatial relationships. If value structure collapses when colour is removed, the colour is carrying structure it should not need to carry — the value layer needs strengthening.

### Print or display at final size
View your image at the size and medium it is actually intended for. For screen delivery, view at 100% zoom on the display that most resembles the delivery environment. For print, produce a test print at final size. Compression artefacts, resolution problems, and detail that looked fine at full working size can become visible or disappear entirely at final delivery size.

### Fresh eyes
Step away from the image for at least several hours — overnight is better. Return and look at it before working on anything else in that session. Fresh eyes remove the familiarity that accumulates during sustained work and make it easier to see the image as a new viewer would. Note the first thing your eye lands on, the first question that arises, and anything that feels unclear.

### Compare against the brief
Return to the written brief you created at the start of the project (style register, lighting idea, viewpoint, background treatment, output goal). Check each decision: does the current state of the image reflect what was planned? Note any divergence between the brief and what is on the canvas and decide whether to correct the image or formally update the brief.

## What the Agent Can Help With

The agent's permitted actions are limited to the following. They do not include aesthetic verdicts.

**Describing what is present.** When vision input is active, the agent can describe what it observes — the position of light and dark patches, where the sharpest edges sit, what the value relationships look like, where the highest contrast is. A description states what is there, not whether it is good.

**Mapping observations to known patterns.** The agent can match an observation to the failure-mode table in the reference corpus. For example: if observed dark patches are scattered evenly across the image rather than gathered into a few large areas, that matches the value massing failure pattern. The agent names the pattern and describes the corrective action — it does not say the image is bad.

**Identifying technical issues.** The agent can identify objective technical properties: edge hardness relative to the focal area, value range, contrast levels at different scales, consistency of light direction across cast shadows, and whether value structure holds after desaturation. These are observations, not quality verdicts.

**Providing technique steps.** When a failure-mode pattern is identified, the agent provides the technique steps that address it. The steps come from the corpus. The user decides whether to apply them.

## What Is the User's Territory

The following are yours as the artist. The agent does not enter this territory.

- **Whether the image is good.** The agent does not rate, rank, score, or grade the work. If you ask for a rating, the agent will redirect into a technique block instead of providing one.
- **Whether the image is finished.** The definition of done in the reference corpus provides a checklist — but whether the image meets it to your satisfaction is your judgment.
- **Aesthetic verdicts of any kind.** Whether a colour palette is beautiful, whether a composition is striking, whether a stylistic choice is successful — these are your decisions. The agent describes what it observes; you decide what to do with that observation.
- **Which corrective actions to take.** The agent identifies patterns and offers technique steps. You choose which, if any, to apply.

## Common Mistakes in the Revision Process

**Running checks too early.** Checks like the squint test and thumbnail test are most useful once the value structure and composition are substantially established. Running them during the rough block-in stage produces observations that will become irrelevant once more of the image is resolved.

**Checking at full zoom only.** Most of these checks require zooming out or changing viewing conditions. Checking only at the working zoom — typically 50–100% — makes it easy to miss proportion problems, balance errors, and focal hierarchy failures that are only visible at smaller sizes.

**Skipping the flip.** The horizontal flip is one of the most reliable checks available, and it is frequently skipped because it feels disorienting. The disorientation is the point. If you find yourself reluctant to flip, that reluctance is a signal that the check is overdue.

**Acting on every observation immediately.** The revision process works best when you run all the checks, write down every observation, and then decide which issues to address and in what order. Jumping to fix the first problem noticed often introduces new problems before the full picture of what needs attention is clear.

**Revising indefinitely without a stop condition.** Decide before you begin revision how many passes you will allow. Unbounded revision is a common cause of unfinished work. When the definition-of-done checklist is met and the checks no longer surface new structural issues, the piece is ready for export — even if it still feels improvable.

**Confusing a technique problem with an aesthetic preference.** The checks above identify structural and technical issues. If a check reveals that value structure collapses under desaturation, that is a technique problem with a known corrective action. If you simply dislike the colour palette, that is an aesthetic preference — the checks do not address it, and the agent does not evaluate it.

## Plain-English Guide

> **Who this is for:** Someone opening a digital painting app for the first time with no formal art training.

### What are critique methods?

Critique methods are a set of ways of looking at your own painting that help you see it more clearly. When you spend a long time working on a painting, you stop seeing it the way a fresh viewer would — your eye skips over problems because it has memorised where everything is. The checks described here are techniques for breaking that familiarity so you can see your own work more honestly. None of them require any special skill. They are all just different ways of looking.

An important note about how the agent uses this: the agent can describe what it observes and point to known patterns in a reference guide. It does not decide whether your painting is good. That call belongs to you.

### The key ideas, without the jargon

**Blurring your eyes on purpose.** Half-close your eyes so the image blurs. The fine detail disappears and you are left with only the big light and dark patches. Think of squinting at a brightly lit street through almost-closed eyelids: you can still tell where the cars are, where the road is, where the sky is — because those things are big patches of different brightness. Your painting should do the same thing when blurred. If you can still tell what the main subject is when your eyes are nearly shut, the light-and-dark structure of your painting is solid.

**Zooming out until it looks small.** Zoom out until the painting is the size of a playing card on your screen. Errors in proportion and balance that are invisible at full size suddenly become obvious at small size, the same way a small bump on an otherwise smooth wall is invisible from a metre away but is easy to see from across the room where you can take in the whole wall at once.

**Flipping the image sideways.** Most painting apps have a button that temporarily mirrors your canvas left-to-right. When you flip, your eye no longer knows where everything is — it has to look at the image fresh. Tilts you stopped noticing, one side feeling more crowded than the other, a face that is slightly off — these things become visible again after a flip because the flipped version looks unfamiliar. Check the image, note what seems wrong, flip back, and fix it.

**Removing all the colour.** There is usually an option in your app to turn the image to black and white temporarily (look for Desaturation, or move the Saturation slider all the way to the left). When the colour is gone, check whether the painting still looks three-dimensional — whether there are still clearly a bright area, a middle area, and a dark area. If the painting flattens to a uniform grey when you remove the colour, the colour was doing work that the light and shadow underneath should have been doing. Fix the light and shadow, then add the colour back.

**Waiting and coming back.** If you step away from a painting for a few hours — go for a walk, eat a meal, sleep on it — and then look at it again, your eye sees it almost the way a stranger would. The first thing your eye lands on when you come back is likely where the painting is drawing attention whether you intended it to or not. The first thing that puzzles you or looks a bit off is likely a real problem. Fresh eyes are one of the most reliable checking tools available and they cost nothing.

**Reading it against your plan.** Before you started the painting, you (ideally) wrote down what you were trying to make — the mood, the lighting, what the scene shows, what size it will be. Look at that plan and look at the painting. Ask: does the painting match what I planned? If something has drifted away from the plan, decide whether to steer the painting back or update the plan. Either is fine — what matters is that the decision is conscious.

### What to actually do to check your own work

1. **Do the squint test.** Sit back from your screen. Half-close your eyes until the image blurs. Can you still see where the main subject is? Is one area clearly brighter or darker than the rest? Write down what you see.

2. **Zoom way out.** In your app's zoom controls, go to 20% or 25%. Look at the painting at that small size. Does the main subject still read clearly? Does one side feel heavier or more crowded? Note what you observe.

3. **Flip the canvas.** Find the "flip horizontal" or "mirror canvas" option (in Procreate: Canvas > Flip Horizontal; in Krita: Image > Mirror Image Horizontally; in Photoshop: Image > Image Rotation > Flip Canvas Horizontal). Look at the flipped version. Notice any tilts, lopsidedness, or proportion issues. Flip back. Fix what you found.

4. **Strip the colour.** In your app, temporarily desaturate the image (in Procreate: use a Gradient Map adjustment in black-and-white mode; in Photoshop or Krita: Image > Adjustments > Desaturate, or add a Hue/Saturation layer and drag Saturation to -100). Look at the grey version. Is it still clearly three-dimensional with a range from dark to light? If everything looks a similar mid-grey, the light and shadow structure needs strengthening. Return the colour and address the underlying light-and-shadow layer.

5. **Step away.** Close the file. Do something else for at least a few hours. Come back and open the file. The first thing your eye lands on is what the painting is actually emphasising. The first uncertainty you feel is a real signal. Write those observations down before you start working.

6. **Check it against your original plan.** Read the brief or notes you wrote at the start. Compare each item to what is on the canvas now. Note any differences. Decide consciously whether to adjust the painting or update the plan.

### Common traps to avoid

**Checking only at the size you always work at.** If you always paint at 50% zoom and you check at 50% zoom, you will miss everything that only becomes visible when small. The thumbnail check and the squint test specifically require you to look at the image differently from how you normally work.

**Skipping the flip because it feels odd.** The flipped version looks strange — that is the point. The strangeness is your brain encountering your painting without the habit of looking at it. If you do not flip, you do not get the benefit. It takes about thirty seconds and costs nothing.

**Fixing problems as soon as you notice them, one at a time.** Run all the checks first, then write down everything you noticed. Then look at the list and decide what order to address things in. Fixing one thing at a time without a list means you often fix something in one part of the image and accidentally create a new problem somewhere else, because you did not yet know what all the problems were.

**Thinking the agent decides when you are done.** The agent can describe what it observes and tell you whether a specific observation matches a known pattern. It cannot tell you the painting is finished or good — that is your call. The checks above will tell you when there are no more structural problems visible. Whether the painting is good enough is a different question, and only you can answer it.
