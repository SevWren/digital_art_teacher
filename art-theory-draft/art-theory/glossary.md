# Glossary

**Status:** Work in progress. Terms are added as the knowledge base grows.

**Atmospheric perspective** — Reduction of contrast, saturation, and detail with distance, often accompanied by a shift toward the colour of the intervening atmosphere.

**Cast shadow** — Shadow projected by a form onto another surface.

**Core shadow** — The darkest region of a form’s own shadow, where the surface turns away from the light and receives minimal reflected light.

**Gesture** — The overall action, weight, and rhythm of a pose, expressed economically before detailed construction.

**Hierarchy** — The ordered ranking of visual importance within an image, usually established by contrast, isolation, or scale.

**Horizon line** — The eye-level line in linear perspective; vanishing points for horizontal edges lie on it.

**Key light** — The primary directional light that defines the main modelling of forms.

**Notan** — The reduction of an image to a small number of value families (commonly two or three) to test compositional structure.

**Occlusion / Contact shadow** — The darkest value where two surfaces meet and block ambient light.

**Primary forms** — Box, cylinder, sphere (and simple variants) used as the starting construction of more complex objects.

**Value family** — A group of related lightness values kept distinct from other groups (e.g., light family vs shadow family).

**Vanishing point** — The point toward which a set of parallel edges appears to converge in linear perspective.

## Plain-English Guide

### What this is

This glossary lists technical terms that appear across the knowledge base. Each entry has its formal definition followed immediately by a plain-English gloss — what the term means in everyday language while you're making a painting. You do not need to memorise the formal definitions; you need to know what each term is pointing at in your image.

### Why it matters for your painting

When a coaching response refers to one of these terms, you need to know what to look for on your canvas. A plain-English version of each term gives you a concrete, observable target rather than an abstract concept.

### The one thing to remember

Every technical term is answering one of two questions: "What am I looking at?" or "What should I check?" When a term feels unfamiliar, ask yourself one of those questions and the plain-English version usually follows.

### Common beginner pitfall

Skipping over an unfamiliar term and assuming it will make sense from context. Terms like "notan" and "occlusion" are specific enough that guessing from context leads to incorrect technique decisions — for example, treating "occlusion" as the same as "cast shadow" when they are different values in different locations.

### Plain-English Glosses

**Atmospheric perspective** — The further away something is, the lighter, less saturated, and fuzzier it appears. Distant mountains look pale, washed-out, and soft compared to rocks close by. That change in clarity with distance is atmospheric perspective.

**Cast shadow** — The dark shape that an object throws onto a nearby surface. Your shadow on the pavement on a sunny day is a cast shadow — it is not on you, it is on the ground beside you.

**Core shadow** — The darkest part of the shadow that sits on the object itself, on the surface that faces away from the light. On a cylinder lit from the left, the core shadow is the band on the right-facing side — not the shadow on the floor, but the dark area on the form.

**Gesture** — The "big move" of a pose: a running figure's forward lean, a dancer's bent knee, the overall tilt and flow before any detail is drawn. If you squint at a rough stick figure, gesture is what the stick figure is doing.

**Hierarchy** — What the viewer looks at first, second, and last. The most important element in the image attracts the eye first, usually by being brighter, more contrasted, or more isolated than everything else. Like a room where one painting has a spotlight on it — everything else recedes.

**Horizon line** — In any scene, the horizon line sits exactly at eye level. Crouch down and it drops. Stand on a table and it rises. All perspective in a scene is organised around this line.

**Key light** — The main light source illuminating the scene. One lamp on the left side of a face is the key light. It determines which surfaces face the light, which face away, and where the shadows fall.

**Notan** — Reducing an image to just two tones — black shapes and white shapes — to test whether the big composition reads clearly before any detail exists. Like checking whether a design works as a logo: if it holds up as a black-and-white silhouette pattern, it has good notan.

**Occlusion / Contact shadow** — The very dark, often sharply edged shadow right where two surfaces touch and block light from reaching between them. The underside of a box sitting on a table: the darkest value is at the line where box meets table. That's occlusion.

**Primary forms** — The simple 3D building blocks used to rough in complex objects: box, cylinder, sphere. A head starts as a sphere; a torso starts as a box. Blocking in with primary forms before adding detail keeps proportions correct and makes lighting decisions easier.

**Value family** — A group of related tones kept consistently apart from another group. The light family contains all the bright areas in the image; the shadow family contains all the dark areas. Keeping those two families distinct — never mixing a light value into the shadows or vice versa — is what makes a painting read clearly as a lit three-dimensional scene.

**Vanishing point (plain English)** — The dot on the horizon where parallel edges appear to converge. Train tracks seem to meet at a point in the distance: that meeting point on the horizon is the vanishing point. Every set of parallel edges in a scene has its own vanishing point somewhere on the horizon line.
