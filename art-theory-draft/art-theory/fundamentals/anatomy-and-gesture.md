# Anatomy and Gesture

**Status:** Work in progress.

For figurative work, anatomy supplies the internal structure that makes poses believable; gesture supplies the overall action, weight, and rhythm that make the figure feel alive. Both are required; anatomy without gesture is stiff, gesture without anatomy is vague.

## Gesture

Gesture is the first, most economical statement of the pose:

- Overall action line or “line of action”
- Weight distribution and balance
- Major directional thrusts of torso, limbs, and head
- Rhythm and flow between forms

Gesture is solved early (stage 04) and remains the reference for all subsequent anatomical refinement.

## Structural Anatomy (Artistic)

Focus on landmarks and mass relationships rather than medical nomenclature:

- Skeletal landmarks that show on the surface (clavicle, ASIS, greater trochanter, knees, elbows, etc.)
- Major muscle groups as form masses that change shape with action
- Joint types and their natural range of motion
- Proportions of the average adult figure as a starting reference, adjusted for the specific character and pose

## Decision Criteria

1. Capture the gesture and weight first; lock the overall action before refining individual limbs.
2. Construct the torso and pelvis as related volumes before attaching limbs.
3. Check foreshortening against the underlying construction rather than against a memorised flat view.
4. Verify that the pose is in balance (or intentionally off-balance) by locating the centre of gravity relative to the support.

## Common Failure Modes

- Stiff, “assembled” figures that lack a unifying action line.
- Limbs attached without regard to the orientation of the torso and pelvis.
- Ignoring weight and balance, so the figure appears to float or tip.
- Over-detailing muscles before the major masses and gesture are correct.

## Plain-English Guide

> **Who this is for:** Someone opening a digital painting app for the first time with no formal art training.

### What is gesture (in drawing)?

Gesture — that means the single sweeping idea of what a whole body is doing before you draw any individual part — is always the first mark you make when drawing a person. Watch someone sprint for a bus. Before your eye registers any individual leg or arm, it catches the whole body tipped forward, stretched diagonally like a falling plank. That full-body lean is the thing to capture first. Every line you draw after that should agree with it: if you lose the lean, the figure looks like it is standing still even if the legs are mid-stride. Think of it as the body's headline. You write the headline first, then fill in the article. The headline for "sprinting" is a forward-falling diagonal. The headline for "relaxed slouch" is a loose S-shaped curve from the top of the head down through the spine. Once that one curved stroke is on the canvas, every arm, leg, and head you add is just supporting evidence for the headline.

### The key ideas, without the jargon

**The body's headline (gesture):** The single flowing stroke that says what the whole body is doing. Draw it before any individual part. Everyday anchor: watch someone sprint for a bus — the whole body is a single forward-falling diagonal before you notice the legs. That diagonal is the headline.

**The spine of the pose (line of action):** One curved or S-shaped stroke drawn from the top of the head all the way down to the feet before anything else. Everyday anchor: hold a flexible plastic ruler at one end and let it droop into a curve. That single curve, end to end, is exactly what this stroke looks like on the canvas. If you draw the head tilted one way, the spine another, and the hips a third, it looks like three different people glued together.

**Natural body curves (C-shape and S-shape):** Almost every relaxed human pose flows in either a simple arc like the letter C, or a reversed double arc like the letter S. Everyday anchor: look at a garden hose lying on the ground after someone used it — it never lies ruler-straight; it settles into a loose S or C. A person standing relaxed does the same thing. Drawing the spine as a perfectly straight vertical line makes the figure look like a toy soldier no matter how natural the arms and legs are.

**Body map (structural anatomy):** The handful of bony bumps that poke through the skin and act as fixed markers — elbows, knees, hip points, collarbones, ankles. Everyday anchor: think of a coat hanger inside a winter jacket. You cannot see the hanger directly, but you can see where its corners tent the fabric at the shoulders. You do not need to know every bone — just where the fixed bump points are. Once those fixed points are in the right place, the skin and muscle between them fill in naturally.

**Surface markers (landmark bones):** The specific hard knobs you can feel on the body's surface — the bony tip of the elbow, the kneecap, the bump at the back of the ankle. Everyday anchor: feel your own elbow tip — it is there whether the arm is bent or straight. Place these fixed knobs first in your drawing. They do not move with the muscle underneath, so getting them right prevents the limb from bending in the wrong place later.

**The squishing effect (foreshortening):** When an arm, leg, or any object points straight toward you, it looks much shorter and more compressed than you would expect. Everyday anchor: hold a cardboard paper-towel tube horizontally in front of you — it looks long. Now tilt one end toward your face. The tube appears to shrink, not because it got shorter but because you are seeing it end-on. A finger pointing straight at a camera looks almost like a circle with a tip. Trust what your eye actually sees — draw the short, compressed shape.

**Chest-and-hips block (torso-pelvis relationship):** The chest and the pelvis are two separate box-like volumes connected at the waist. They can tilt and twist in different directions independently before any muscles or clothing are added. Everyday anchor: take two cardboard boxes and connect them loosely with a short length of rope. Tilt one left while the other stays level. The human torso works exactly this way. Drawing them as one flat rectangle produces a stiff, unnatural figure.

**The outer box first (envelope):** Before drawing any internal detail on a figure, lightly sketch the outermost boundary of the whole shape — as if you were drawing the box the figure would come shipped in. Everyday anchor: think of how jigsaw-puzzle experts build the outer border of straight-edged pieces first before filling in the middle. The outer boundary tells you the total space the figure occupies before you commit to any internal line.

**Vertical check (plumb line):** Drop an imaginary straight vertical line from any point on the figure — say, from the tip of the nose straight down — and see what other body parts that line passes through or lands near. This tells you whether parts are stacked over each other correctly or have drifted sideways. Everyday anchor: a builder uses a plumb bob (a weight on a string) to check whether a wall is truly vertical. You use the same idea to check whether the ankle is directly below the hip in a standing pose.

**Measuring with your own drawing (comparative measurement):** Use a length already in your drawing as a measuring stick to check other parts, rather than guessing. The head is the most convenient ruler: mark how tall it is, then count how many of those head-heights stack up to reach the feet. Everyday anchor: a tailor uses a tape measure to compare one measurement against another — sleeve length vs. shoulder width. You do the same with the parts of your own drawing.

**Weight and stability:** Where the body's bulk sits relative to the feet determines whether a figure looks grounded or about to fall. Everyday anchor: stand on one foot and lean slowly sideways. At a certain point you must hop or put your foot down, because your weight has moved past what your one foot can cover. A figure drawn with all the body mass shifted far to one side but both feet planted on the other side looks like it should be falling over.

### What to actually do in your software

1. **Start with one large, loose stroke.** In your app (Procreate, Krita, Photoshop, Photopea), create a new layer and set the brush to a large, soft round brush — something big enough that a single stroke covers most of the canvas height. With a quick, single motion, draw one flowing line from where the top of the head will be down through the spine to the feet. Do not lift or slow down. This stroke should take under two seconds. This is your headline.

2. **Block in the two torso boxes.** Still on the rough sketch layer, draw a rough egg or brick shape for the chest and a slightly smaller shape for the pelvis. Tilt them to match the pose — if the figure is leaning, both boxes lean; if the hips are tilted, one corner of the pelvis box is higher than the other. Keep the opacity of this layer around 50–60% so it reads as a guide, not finished art.

3. **Place the fixed bony markers next.** On the same sketch layer or a new one directly above it, dot in the hard fixed points: the two hip bumps at the front of the pelvis, the tip of each elbow, both kneecaps, and the collarbones. These do not need to be precise circles — small crosshairs or dots are enough. Check that the two hip bumps are at different heights if the hips are tilted, and that the elbow tip is behind the arm, not in front.

4. **Add the limbs last, then check balance.** Attach the arms to the chest box and the legs to the pelvis box. Once all limbs are roughed in, drop an imaginary vertical line from the center of the torso to the floor. In your software, you can use the straight-line tool or ruler guide at 90 degrees to do this literally. Check whether that vertical line lands between the feet. If the pose is meant to look stable and grounded, the line must land between or directly over the feet.

### Common traps to avoid

- **Drawing body parts first, pose never.** Adding a detailed hand or face before the overall lean of the body is locked in is the single most common cause of stiff, disconnected figures. The body ends up looking like separate pieces joined together rather than one person in motion. Always lay down the single flowing headline stroke before touching any individual part.

- **Straight spine, stiff figure.** Drawing the spine as a perfectly vertical line — or any perfectly straight line — makes the figure look frozen at attention even if the arms and legs are in a relaxed position. Natural poses always involve some curve or lean through the spine. If your figure looks like a toy soldier, check whether the main stroke through the body has any curve at all.

- **Both hip dots at the same height.** In almost any pose except rigid standing at attention, the two bony hip points at the front of the pelvis are at different heights — one side is higher when the weight is on one leg, or when the figure is mid-step. Drawing them at exactly the same height makes every pose look stiff and symmetrical regardless of how the legs are positioned.

- **Drawing the arm at its "real" length when it points at you.** When an arm is reaching toward the viewer, it looks much shorter on the canvas than you expect — sometimes only a third of its actual length. The instinct to "correct" this and draw it longer produces an arm that looks broken or impossible. Trust what your eye sees. Draw the short, compressed shape.
