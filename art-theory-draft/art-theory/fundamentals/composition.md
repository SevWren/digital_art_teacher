# Composition

**Status:** Work in progress.

Composition is the arrangement of visual elements so that the image communicates hierarchy, guides the eye, and supports the intended statement. It is solved most effectively at the thumbnail stage and remains the primary reference for every later decision.

## Core Components

### Elements
- Line, shape, value mass, colour mass, texture, space (positive/negative), scale, and direction.

### Principles (relationships among elements)
- **Hierarchy / Emphasis** — one dominant area that receives the strongest contrast or isolation.
- **Balance** — distribution of visual weight (symmetrical, asymmetrical, or radial).
- **Rhythm & Repetition** — repeated intervals that create movement or stability.
- **Unity & Variety** — coherence without monotony.
- **Proportion & Scale** — relative size relationships that reinforce hierarchy.
- **Contrast** — differences in value, size, direction, texture, or colour that create focus.

### Gestalt Grouping
Proximity, similarity, continuity, closure, and figure–ground relationships determine how the eye first organises the image.

## Practical Decision Criteria

1. **Value notan first.** Reduce the image to two or three value families. If the notan is weak, later colour and detail will not rescue the design.
2. **Silhouette readability.** The major shapes should remain legible when the image is reduced to pure black and white.
3. **Entry and path.** Identify where the eye enters and the path it is intended to travel. Avoid accidental equal attractions that compete with the focal area.
4. **Negative space.** Treat empty areas as active shapes that support or contradict the positive forms.
5. **Cropping and edge.** The four edges of the frame are part of the composition; intentional cropping can strengthen or destroy hierarchy.

## Common Failure Modes

- Multiple competing centres of equal contrast.
- Tangents (shapes that touch edges or each other in ways that flatten space).
- Uniform distribution of detail that eliminates hierarchy.
- Solving colour or rendering before the value structure is locked.

## Plain-English Guide

> **Who this is for:** Someone opening Procreate, Krita, Photopea, or Photoshop for the first time with no formal art training.

### What is composition?

Composition is the decision about where to put everything in your image — and why. Before you pick a single colour or paint a single detail, you are making choices about what goes where, how big it is, and what the viewer will notice first. Think of it like arranging furniture in a room before any guests arrive: you decide where the sofa goes, where the lamp goes, where you want people to sit. A painting works the same way. Get the arrangement right early and all the detail work that comes later will feel solid and intentional. Get the arrangement wrong and no amount of careful rendering will fix it — you will have spent hours on a wobbly foundation.

### The key ideas, without the jargon

**What the viewer notices first, second, and third (hierarchy — that means the order things grab attention):** Look at any newspaper front page. The biggest, boldest headline is what your eye lands on first. The photo is second. The small article text is third. Nobody consciously decided to read in that order — the sizes and boldness made the decision for them. A painting works identically: the brightest thing, or the sharpest thing, or the most isolated thing, gets noticed first. You get to decide what that is.

**Making the image feel stable, not tippy (balance):** Think of a see-saw with two children of different sizes. One heavy child on the right and a light child on the left tips the see-saw down on the right — but if the light child scoots farther from the centre, the see-saw levels out. A painting works the same way: a large dark area close to the middle can be balanced by a small bright detail near the edge, because brightness attracts the eye strongly even at small sizes.

**A beat or pulse that moves the eye (rhythm and repetition):** Watch telephone poles passing by through a car window on a highway. Your eye skips from pole to pole in a regular beat — pole, gap, pole, gap. That regularity creates a sense of motion even in a still image. Repeated shapes, repeated colours, or repeated intervals in a painting create the same beat. When the beat is broken deliberately, that interruption becomes the focal point.

**Making the whole image feel like it belongs together (unity):** Imagine a jigsaw puzzle where every piece came from the same box. Now imagine someone snuck in five pieces from a different puzzle — they might fill a gap, but the colour and style look jarring. A painting achieves this "same-puzzle" quality when all the objects are lit by the same light source, share a consistent level of detail, and feel like they exist in the same space.

**Differences that create interest and focus (contrast):** Imagine a page of grey text on a white background. Now imagine one sentence printed in bold black. That bold sentence jumps off the page because it is surrounded by lighter, less bold text. In a painting, placing a very dark area right next to a very bright area creates the same jump — the eye goes to that boundary first.

**How your eyes automatically clump things together (gestalt — that means how the brain groups nearby things without you deciding to):** A flock of birds looks like one moving smudge, not 500 separate birds — your eyes automatically lump nearby things into one blob before you see any detail. In a painting, three characters standing close together will read as "a group of people" before the viewer looks closely enough to count them.

**How clearly your main subject stands out from the background (figure-ground — that means object-versus-background pop):** Think of a black chess piece sitting on a white table. The piece pops instantly — your eye goes straight to it because it is a different brightness from the surface it sits on. Now imagine a dark grey chess piece on a dark grey table: the piece disappears, it melts into the table. Your main subject needs enough difference in brightness or colour from what is behind it to pop the same way.

**The breathing room around your subject (negative space — that means the empty-looking areas that are actually shapes too):** Look at a silhouette of a person's profile cut from black paper and glued to a white card. The white areas that wrap around the profile are also shapes with their own outlines. If those white shapes are interesting and varied, the whole card looks designed. If the face is shoved into one corner and all the white clumps awkwardly on one side, the card feels lopsided. The open space around your subject is always a shape you are actively designing, whether you think about it or not.

**The dark-and-light skeleton (notan — that means reducing your image to two or three flat blobs of dark and light to test the big picture before adding any detail):** Squint hard at any photograph until everything blurs into a handful of dark blobs and bright blobs. If those blobs make an interesting pattern even when completely blurred, the photo has a strong skeleton. If the blobs all seem the same size scattered evenly everywhere, the photo looks restless and crowded. A painting works the same way — before colour or detail, those dark-and-light blobs need to tell a story on their own.

**Grouping darks together and lights together (value massing — that means keeping all the dark parts in a few large connected patches, not scattered everywhere):** Think of how a lit window looks from outside at night: dark sky above, dark ground below, and one rectangle of warm light in the middle. Three big blobs — two dark, one light. That window reads instantly from far away. Now imagine someone had punched 50 tiny holes in a wall with light leaking through each hole randomly: the same total amount of light, but chaotic and hard to read. Keeping your darks gathered into a few confident patches — rather than scattered as tiny specks — gives your image that same clean readability.

**Recognisable outline (silhouette readability — that means whether your main subject is instantly recognisable as a distinct shape with all internal detail removed):** Think of the Batman logo — the black bat shape on a yellow background. Cover the yellow and just look at the black shape. You can still tell it is a bat immediately, even though it is just a flat black blob with no face or texture. That is a strong outline. Now imagine a character whose raised sword arm sits at the same angle as their body — the arm and body merge into one indistinct blob. That is a weak outline. The silhouette needs to read clearly before any detail is added.

**Accidental touching edges (tangents — that means two shapes in the image that accidentally just barely touch or line up, which flattens the picture and snags the eye):** Imagine a photo of a friend standing in a park with a lamppost directly behind their head, its top edge perfectly meeting the top of their skull. It looks like the lamppost is growing out of their head. The same thing happens in a painting when a sword tip just barely grazes the frame edge, or a horizon line runs exactly through a character's shoulder. The eye snags on the accidental alignment. The fix is always small: either clearly overlap the two things, or clearly leave space between them. Never a graze.

**Where the viewer enters and travels through your image (eye path):** Watch someone read a restaurant menu for the first time. Their eye almost always starts at the top and moves across and down — the designer reinforced this with a bold header near the top. Then the eye drifts through categories, landing on anything boxed or bolded. A painting has the same kind of path: where the eye enters (usually the most contrasting spot) and then lines, edges, and bright patches that carry it from one area to the next. If those paths lead off the edge of the canvas, the viewer's attention escapes and never returns to the subject.

### What to actually do in your software

1. **Start with a tiny thumbnail, not a full-size canvas.** Create a new canvas at about 200 × 200 pixels (or just a small corner of a large canvas). Use a hard round brush with black, white, and one grey. Rough in your whole image as flat blobs — no detail, no colour. Squint at it. If the dark blobs and light blobs make an interesting arrangement, you have a solid foundation. If everything blurs into a single muddy medium grey, rearrange the blobs before scaling up.

2. **Put your main subject off-centre and check that it is clearly the brightest or sharpest thing.** In most programs, turn on the Rule of Thirds overlay (in Procreate: Canvas > Drawing Guide > 2D Grid; in Photoshop: View > Show > Grid). Place your main subject near one of the four crossing points rather than dead centre. Then temporarily desaturate the whole canvas (Photoshop: Image > Adjustments > Desaturate; Procreate: Adjustments > Hue, Saturation, Brightness, drag Saturation to zero) and confirm that your main subject is clearly the lightest or darkest thing near it — not the same grey as everything else.

3. **Check your outline clarity.** On a new layer above everything else, use the Paint Bucket (fill tool) or a large brush to paint your main subject as a flat black blob, ignoring all internal detail. Zoom out. Can you immediately tell what it is? If the arms merge with the body, or a weapon merges with the background, go back and separate those parts — move them to a different angle or add a sliver of contrasting colour between them.

4. **Hunt for accidental edge touches before adding any detail.** Zoom out to about 25–30% so the whole image is small on screen. Look for any two things whose edges just barely graze each other — a character touching the frame edge, a horizon line running exactly through a shoulder, two objects kissing without overlapping. Each one you find: nudge one object a few pixels so the edges either clearly overlap or clearly have a gap. Fix these before investing time in rendering.

### Common traps to avoid

- **Putting everything at the same brightness.** When every part of the image has roughly the same contrast and level of detail, the viewer's eye has nowhere to land — it wanders around without settling. Decide on one spot as your focal point and make it clearly the brightest or most detailed. Then quietly lower everything else one notch.

- **Scattering small dark and bright patches all over the canvas.** If your darks are scattered in dozens of small spots rather than gathered into a few large areas, the image will look busy and hard to read from a distance — like 50 tiny holes in a wall instead of one clean window. Gather your darks into two or three connected patches before adding any detail.

- **Adding colour and detail before the dark-and-light arrangement works.** It is tempting to jump into colour and rendering right away. But if the underlying arrangement of dark blobs and light blobs is weak, no colour or detail will fix it. The colour and rendering sit on top of that skeleton — if the skeleton is wrong, you will need to undo hours of work. Check the skeleton first, always.

- **Letting lines and edges lead the eye off the edge of the canvas.** Roads, outstretched arms, fences, and value gradients all act like arrows pointing the eye in a direction. If those arrows point toward the edge of the canvas, the viewer's attention escapes and never comes back to your subject. Before finalising the composition, trace the direction of every major edge and make sure it curves back toward — not away from — your main subject.
