# Stage 11 — Critique, Revision & Export

**Status:** Work in progress.

Stage 11 is the closing gate of the pipeline. You return to the goal you wrote at the very start, check whether the finished image actually achieves it, make any targeted fixes that the check reveals, and then save and export the files correctly. This stage is not about adding more; it is about verifying what is already there and getting the files into the right shape for use.

---

## What You Do at This Stage

**Run the definition-of-done check.** Work through the following five questions in order. Each one is a pass/fail gate, not a matter of taste.

1. **Structural consistency.** Do the proportions and construction hold up at both full size and thumbnail size? Do forms sit convincingly in space with no shapes that appear to float or lean?
2. **Value structure.** Temporarily desaturate the image (most software has a black-and-white preview mode). Does the image still read clearly in grey? Are the most important areas the lightest or highest-contrast, and do less important areas recede?
3. **Colour coherence.** Do the colour relationships support the lighting idea you chose? Does the shadow colour temperature agree with the light colour temperature throughout?
4. **Edge hierarchy.** Are the sharpest, hardest edges at the focal point, and do edges soften toward the edges and background? Does the eye land where it should?
5. **Technical requirements.** Does the file meet the output goal stated in the brief — correct colour space, correct resolution or pixel dimensions, correct format?

**Review against the brief.** Go back to the goal you wrote in Stage 1. Read it as if seeing the image for the first time. Does the image achieve what the brief asked for? This is the user's call to make, not the agent's. The agent can describe what is present; only the user can decide whether it is done.

**Make targeted revisions.** If the definition-of-done check flags a problem, fix that specific thing and check again. Do not open the image to general improvement at this point — the pipeline is designed so that large problems are solved early. Revision here means fixing what the checklist found, not restarting.

**Save the master file.** Keep the native layered file intact — all layer groups separate and clearly named, nothing flattened, at full resolution and bit depth. This is the master. Never flatten the master.

**Export a delivery copy.** Create a separate, flattened copy for the output goal. Apply the correct colour space (see below). Save this export in a clearly named export folder, separate from the master.

**Archive the project folder.** Collect master file, exports, reference images, brief or notes, and any version files into a single clearly named folder with a date. This is the archive. A consistent structure prevents lost work.

---

## Stage Sign-off Condition

The image satisfies all five points of the definition-of-done check above. A master layered file exists and has not been flattened. A separate delivery copy exists in the correct format and colour space for the output goal. The project folder is archived with a clear name and date.

---

## Common Failure Modes

**Treating "I think it looks fine" as the definition of done.** The five-point check replaces that feeling with a concrete verification. Run through it in order, not all at once.

**Flattening the master.** Once the master is flattened, individual layer groups cannot be revised. Always flatten a copy, never the original.

**Saving one file for everything.** A single file used as both the working master and the delivery copy will eventually end up flattened, resized, or colour-shifted in a way that is hard to undo. Keep them separate from the start.

**Exporting in the wrong colour space.** A file in a wide working space such as Adobe RGB that is exported without converting to sRGB will look dull or differently tinted on most screens and in most browsers. sRGB is the safe default for screen delivery. If print is the target, use the profile the printer specifies.

**Endless tweaking without a stopping condition.** Without a definition-of-done checklist, it is possible to keep making small changes indefinitely because "something still feels off." The checklist provides a stopping condition: once all five pass, the image is done.

**Opening revision as a general improvement session.** At Stage 11, revisions should be targeted responses to specific items the checklist flagged — not a new round of painting. General improvement belongs at earlier stages.

---

## Connections to Other Stages

- **Stage 1 (Brief & Ideation):** The brief written at Stage 1 is the primary reference for the critique check. If no brief was written, the definition-of-done check has no target — this is the most common reason Stage 11 stalls.
- **Stages 6–10 (Value through Polish):** The five-point check maps directly onto decisions made in these stages. If the desaturated value check fails, the problem originates at Stage 6 or 7. If the edge hierarchy check fails, the problem originates at Stage 9 or 10. Targeted revision means returning to the governing stage, not reworking the whole image.
- **Stage 11 is final.** There is no next stage. The output of this stage is a safely archived project folder containing a verified master and a correctly formatted delivery file.

## Plain-English Guide

> **Who this is for:** Someone who has worked through the previous stages and is wondering how to know when the painting is actually finished, and what to do with the file once it is.

### What is the Critique and Export stage?

You know that moment when you step back from a piece of furniture you have just assembled and check whether it is level, whether all the screws are tight, and whether it matches what the instructions said you were building? That is this stage. You are not building anything new — you are checking that what you built is actually what you set out to build, that it is solid, and that it is ready to go in the room.

The word "critique" sounds like someone is about to tell you whether your painting is good or bad. It is not that. Critique here means a specific, systematic check against a written list. It is much closer to a mechanic's pre-drive checklist than to an opinion. The checklist is the same every time. Your taste and judgment are what decide whether the image is done — the checklist just makes sure you have not accidentally skipped something structural.

### Key ideas, without the jargon

**The brief is the finish line.** Before you can decide whether a painting is done, you need to remember what it was supposed to do. Dig out the goal you wrote at the very beginning — even just one sentence. Read the image against it. Does this painting do what you said it needed to do, for the person you said it was for, with the feeling you said it should give? That question is yours to answer, not the assistant's.

**The five-point check is not about taste.** Each of the five checks is structural — it asks whether the image works at a technical level: can you read it in black and white? do the edges lead the eye to the right place? does it meet the size and format requirements? These are verifiable, not subjective. If one fails, it flags a specific area to fix. If all five pass, the image is technically ready. Whether it is right for you is still your call.

**Revising means fixing what the check found — nothing else.** At this point in the pipeline, the big decisions are locked. Revision here is targeted, like adjusting a single loose shelf rather than rebuilding the whole bookcase. If the desaturated check shows that the focal point is not reading clearly, go fix that. Do not use it as an invitation to repaint the whole piece.

**"Finished" needs a definition, or it never happens.** Without a checklist, it is easy to keep touching a painting for weeks. The five-point check gives you a condition that, once met, means you can put the file down. Finishing is a skill, not a feeling.

**Two files, not one.** Once the painting is done, you need two versions of it.
- The **master file** is your layered working file — every group of layers still separate, nothing flattened, saved in the software's own format (PSD, CLIP, KRA, etc.). Keep this. Never flatten it. It is the source of truth you can return to if anything needs to change later.
- The **delivery copy** is a separate, flattened file exported for the specific use: a JPEG or PNG at the right pixel size for uploading, or a TIFF at print resolution. This is what you send or post. Keep it in a separate folder from the master.

**Colour space: sRGB for screens, wider for print.** When you export for anything that will be viewed on a screen — a website, social media, a portfolio, an email — export in the sRGB colour space. sRGB is the recipe that browsers, phones, and most screens understand. If your file is in a different colour space and you do not convert it, some programs will translate it automatically and others will not, and your colours can look duller or differently tinted on someone else's screen. For print, use the colour profile the printer specifies; when in doubt, ask the print service.

**Archive the whole project, not just the final file.** Create one folder for this project containing everything: the master file, the exports, the reference images, the brief notes, any sketch files. Name it with a date and a short description. Saving only the final flat export means that three months from now you will not be able to edit the layers, find the references, or remember what the brief was. The folder is the project.

### What to do in your software

1. **Run the five-point check before doing anything else.** Desaturate the image temporarily and check the value structure. Then re-enable colour and check colour coherence and edge hierarchy. Write down anything that fails.
2. **Fix only what the check flagged.** Work on the specific area that failed. Check it again. Move on.
3. **Compare the finished image to the brief.** Read the brief. Look at the image. If you are satisfied it does what the brief asked, the painting is done. If you are not, identify what specifically is missing and fix that — not "everything."
4. **Save the master.** File > Save (in your software's native format). Do not flatten anything. Check that your layer groups are clearly named.
5. **Export the delivery copy.** File > Export As (or equivalent). Choose JPEG or PNG for screen use. In the export dialogue, confirm the colour space is sRGB. Name the file clearly and save it to your Exports folder, not your Working folder.
6. **Archive the project folder.** Move the whole project folder — master, exports, references, brief — somewhere safe and give it a clear name with today's date.

### Common traps

**Critiquing as a feelings exercise.** "Does this look good?" is not a check you can complete. "Does it read clearly in black and white?" is. Run the checklist, not the feeling.

**Skipping the brief review.** The brief is the only objective standard you have for whether the image is done. If you skip it, you are deciding the image is finished by feel, which tends to mean either never finishing or stopping too early.

**Exporting the master instead of a copy.** If you export from the master file and that export process resizes or flattens the file, you may lose layers permanently. Always export from a copy or use Export As rather than Save As when saving the delivery version.

**Opening the image again the next day to keep going.** If the five-point check passes and the brief is met, put the file in the archive folder and move on. The archive folder is what makes it feel final — once it is in there with a date on the name, it is done.
