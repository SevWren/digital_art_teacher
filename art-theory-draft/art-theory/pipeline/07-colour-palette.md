# Stage 07 — Colour & Palette

**Status:** Work in progress.

Stage 07 is where you bring colour into a painting that already works in grey. The goal is not to replace the light-and-dark structure you built in Stage 06 — it is to support and enrich it. Every colour decision at this stage is checked against the grey version to make sure the value hierarchy still holds.

---

## What You Do at This Stage

- Choose a small, deliberate set of colours (a restricted palette) that fits your lighting idea, and commit to it before painting.
- Block in the main local colours — the base colour each object actually is under neutral light — across the whole image at low detail.
- Shift the lit areas toward the temperature of your light source (warm key light = cooler shadows; cool key light = warmer shadows).
- Separate local colour, light colour, and shadow colour so each area is doing the right job rather than being one flat, even tone.
- Record your palette as named swatches so you can match it exactly in a future session.
- Check the whole image in greyscale after colouring; if the value pattern has changed, adjust the colours until the grey structure is restored.

---

## Stage Sign-off Condition

Local colours are blocked across the full image. The palette is recorded and locked. A greyscale check confirms that the value hierarchy from Stage 06 still holds. Colour temperature shifts are legible: lit areas and shadow areas feel like they belong to the same light source but sit at opposite ends of the warm-cool scale.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Applying colour before the value design is locked.** Colour is visually loud and will hide structural weaknesses. If Stage 06 is not signed off, colour will make the problem harder to diagnose, not easier.
- **Equal saturation (vividness) across the whole image.** When every area is equally vivid, the eye has no reason to land anywhere in particular. The focal area should carry the highest saturation; everything else should be quieter.
- **No dominant temperature.** When warm and cool areas are distributed without a clear leader, the image feels unsettled. Decide which temperature is in charge; let the other appear only as a controlled contrast.
- **Ignoring simultaneous contrast.** A colour that looks correct in isolation can look wrong once surrounded by its neighbours. Check each area in context, not in isolation.
- **Painting local colour only, ignoring light colour and shadow colour.** Objects in real light are never a single flat colour. The lit side picks up warmth from the light source; the shadow side picks up cooler ambient or reflected light. Painting only the base colour produces a flat, airless result.
- **No palette record.** Starting the next session without a saved record of your swatches means inconsistent colour choices that gradually drift from session to session.

## Plain-English Guide

> **Who this is for:** A hobbyist digital artist with no formal art training, adding colour to a painting for the first time or trying to understand why their colours are not working.

---

### What is the Colour & Palette stage?

You have already painted a version of your image in shades of grey — you know where the light falls, which parts of the image are bright and which are dark, and the main subject reads clearly even when you squint. Stage 07 is the moment you bring colour in. But you are not starting over. You are placing colour on top of a structure that already works, the same way you might tint a black-and-white photograph: the light and dark pattern stays in charge, and the colour makes it richer.

The job at this stage is not to make things "look coloured." It is to make a deliberate set of colour choices — a small, controlled team of colours — that support the mood and lighting of the image rather than fighting it.

---

### Key ideas, without the jargon

**Why colour comes after grey, not instead of it**

Colour is visually loud. A bold red grabs your eye so forcefully that it is easy to think the red is the problem when actually the underlying light-and-dark structure is what is wrong. By solving light and dark in grey first, you separate two jobs that would otherwise get tangled together. Once the grey version reads clearly, adding colour cannot break it — it can only make it more convincing.

Think of it like mixing a cake batter before choosing the flavour of icing. The batter structure is what makes the cake hold together. The icing is added on top and makes it more appealing. If you put icing on raw batter, the cake collapses and the icing gets the blame.

**Local colour, light colour, shadow colour — the three separate things an object is**

When you look at a red apple sitting in sunshine:

- The part facing the sun is not simply "red." The warm sunlight tints it toward orange-yellow. This is the **light colour** — the local red modified by the warmth of the light source.
- The part facing away from the sun, sitting in shadow, is not simply "dark red." It picks up cooler light from the open sky and nearby surfaces. This is the **shadow colour** — the local red modified by the cool ambient environment.
- The zone in the middle, where neither the direct light nor the deep shadow is strongest, is closest to the "true" red you would describe to someone in a plain, white-lit room. This is the **local colour** — the object's own inherent colour.

Beginner paintings often look flat because they paint only one of these: "it is a red apple, so I will paint it red." Real objects always show all three at once. Separating them is what makes a painted object look solid and convincingly lit.

**Warm light, cool shadows — and why this matters**

The most useful single rule about colour and light: when the main light source is warm (sunlight, candlelight, an incandescent lamp), the shadows lean cool. When the light source is cool (overcast sky, fluorescent light, a blue LED), the shadows lean warm.

Think of a candle in a dark room. The skin and surfaces directly lit by the flame glow orange-yellow. The shadows on the far side of a face, away from the flame, shift toward cooler grey-blue. Both observations come from the same real-world scene. Painting them this way — warm lit side, cool shadow side — immediately makes the scene feel three-dimensional and believable.

The reverse can also be deliberately chosen. A painting lit by a cool, overcast sky might have slightly warm shadows — warm because the ground or nearby surfaces bounce warmer reflected light back into the shadow areas. The principle is the same: lit and shadow are always at different temperatures.

**Keeping the palette small — palette lock**

Before you start adding colour, choose your set. A working palette of four to six colours — a light warm colour, a light cool colour, a dark warm colour, a dark cool colour, and one or two accent colours — is enough for most images. Once you choose them, commit to them. Picking colours freely as you go means each area gets a slightly different decision, and the image gradually starts to feel like it was painted in several different lighting conditions.

Think of the way a professional decorator brings three or four coordinated paint samples and asks you to pick from that range. The range is coordinated on purpose: the colours are designed to live together. If you could pick freely from every colour in the catalogue, it would be much easier to accidentally put colours together that fight each other. Your restricted palette is your coordinated range.

Once you have your palette, save it as named swatches. In Procreate, use the palette panel. In Krita or Photopea, save a custom swatches set. In Photoshop, use the Swatches panel. Label the swatches with their role: "warm light," "cool shadow," "local skin," etc. This is your palette record, and it means your next session can pick up exactly where this one left off.

**How colours shift depending on their neighbours**

Place the same grey square on an orange background and on a blue background. The grey on the orange looks slightly cool and bluish. The grey on the blue looks slightly warm and orangish. The grey itself is identical in both cases — only its surroundings changed. Your eyes automatically compare every colour to whatever is next to it, and that comparison changes what you perceive.

This matters practically: if an area in your painting looks wrong, the problem may not be that area. It may be what is surrounding it. Before repainting, temporarily cover the surrounding area with a flat grey on a new layer and look at the area in isolation. Often the "problem" colour looks perfectly fine on its own.

**Colour pecking order — the most important area gets the most vivid colour**

Vividness only works as a signal when it is rare. A single neon-lit carousel in an otherwise softly lit fairground immediately draws the eye. Every ride blazing at full brightness simultaneously produces visual noise where nothing stands out.

Your focal area — the thing you want the viewer to look at first — should carry the highest colour vividness. Everything else should be quieter. Pull back the vividness of the background and supporting areas (using the Saturation slider in Hue/Saturation, or by adding grey to the colour mixture). The focal area will feel more present and important without any change to the focal area itself.

---

### What to do in your software

1. **Keep your greyscale version visible.** Before doing anything, duplicate your Stage 06 grey layer group and lock it. Keep it visible in a corner of your canvas or on a second viewport. Every colour decision should be checked against it.

2. **Block local colours first.** Create a new layer group above your grey structure. Set the blending mode of the group to "Color" (in Photoshop, Procreate, or Photopea) or "Hue" (in Krita). Inside this group, paint flat, even colour over each major area — the sky, the ground, skin, clothing, and so on. At this point you are only deciding which colour family each area belongs to. Keep it simple and flat.

3. **Add temperature shifts on a separate layer.** Create a new layer above the local colour layer, set to "Color" blending mode at 20–40% opacity. Paint a warm tone (orange-yellow) over the areas directly hit by your light source. Paint a cool tone (blue-violet) over the shadow areas. Toggle the layer on and off to see the difference. Adjust opacity until the shift is believable but not garish.

4. **Save your palette as swatches.** Open the swatches or colour palette panel in your software. Add the exact colours you used and label them. Take a screenshot of the swatch panel or save the palette file. This is your palette record.

5. **Run a greyscale check.** Add a Hue/Saturation adjustment layer at the very top of your layer stack, pull Saturation to -100, and look at the result. Compare it to your Stage 06 grey version. If the dark-and-light pattern has shifted — if an important area has become harder to read — adjust the value of the relevant colour until the grey check matches. Remove the adjustment layer when done.

6. **Check the saturation hierarchy.** With the full colour version visible, squint at it. The focal area should be the most vivid part of the image. If background or secondary areas are equally vivid, add a Hue/Saturation adjustment layer with a mask that protects the focal area and pull saturation down on everything else.

---

### Common traps

**Adding colour to a grey version that was not actually finished.** If the grey version was "good enough" rather than genuinely signed off, colour will make its weaknesses invisible until Stage 09 when fixing them becomes expensive. Use the Stage 06 sign-off condition before moving here.

**Painting the whole image at the same colour temperature.** If both the lit surfaces and the shadow surfaces feel warm (or both feel cool), the image loses a dimension. Temperature contrast between light and shadow is one of the main tools that makes a painted surface read as solid. Without it, the image tends to look like a flat illustration.

**Picking colours freely as you go without a locked palette.** If you choose a slightly different warm yellow in session two than in session one, the image slowly becomes inconsistent. The palette record exists to prevent this.

**Treating simultaneous contrast as a defect.** If a skin tone looks wrong in context, resist the instinct to repaint it immediately. Cover the surrounding area temporarily and check whether the skin tone actually needs to change, or whether the surrounding colours are shifting your perception of it. Often the answer is to adjust the neighbour, not the area that looks wrong.

**Pushing saturation up to make things "pop."** The instinct when a colour feels flat is to make it more vivid. But vividness is relative — a colour only pops when it is surrounded by quieter colours. If everything in the image is pushed up together, nothing pops. The fix is almost always to pull quiet areas down, not to push the focal area further up.
