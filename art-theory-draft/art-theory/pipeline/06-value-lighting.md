# Stage 06 — Value & Lighting

**Status:** Work in progress.

Stage 06 is where you paint a small, greyscale version of your composition to test whether the lighting plan works before any colour is introduced. The governing question is: *Is the light design clear and hierarchical?* A solid value study acts as the authority for everything that follows — colour, rendering, and detail all sit on top of this structure. If the greyscale version reads clearly, colour will strengthen it; if it does not, no amount of colour will rescue it.

---

## What You Do at This Stage

- Create a greyscale pass covering the full image: establish the light side, the shadow side (including the core shadow belt and reflected light), cast shadows, and the thin dark line where objects meet the ground.
- Organise your values into two clear groups — a bright team (lit surfaces) and a dark team (shadowed surfaces) — and keep them clearly separate from each other.
- Place the highest contrast in the image at the focal point; all other areas get lower contrast so the eye knows where to go first.
- Confirm that the image reads as three-dimensional and hierarchical when viewed at thumbnail size, with no colour present.
- Lock this greyscale study as a primary reference before moving to Stage 07 (Colour & Palette). Later stages must not silently override it.

---

## Stage Sign-off Condition

**You can move on when:** A resolved greyscale study exists and satisfies both of the following checks:

1. **Readability check.** Squint at the study until it blurs. The subject or focal area still reads clearly as distinct from its surroundings, and supporting areas visibly recede.
2. **Light-logic check.** Every surface facing the chosen light direction is in the bright team; every surface turned away is in the dark team. Cast shadows point in the correct direction. The two teams do not blur into each other.

---

## Common Failure Modes

| Symptom | What is happening | How to address it |
|---|---|---|
| Everything drifts to a flat medium grey | Bright and dark teams have bled into each other — neither team is committed | Use Levels or Curves to push darks darker and lights lighter; force a genuine near-black and a genuine near-white somewhere |
| Forms look flat after colour is added | The value range was too narrow to begin with, or colour work abandoned the greyscale structure | Return to the greyscale study; restore stronger light-to-core-shadow separation before re-adding colour |
| Objects appear to float above the ground | Cast shadow is missing or the contact dark line is absent | Add the dark patch extending from the object's base in the direction opposite the light source, plus a thin near-black line right where the object meets the surface |
| Light direction is inconsistent across the image | Shadow sides and lit sides are not all governed by the same clock-position light source | Lock a single light direction (e.g., "upper left, 10 o'clock"), write it down, and correct every surface that contradicts it |
| Colour looks wrong but the cause is unclear | The problem is almost always in the value structure, not the colour itself | Desaturate the image and inspect the greyscale version; if the structure is solid there, the colour problem is a colour problem; if it is not, fix the structure first |
| Focal point is not the most eye-catching area | Contrast is equal or higher elsewhere, drawing the eye away | Reduce contrast in non-focal areas; ensure the focal point has the single highest brightness-to-darkness jump in the image |

## Plain-English Guide

> **Who this is for:** Someone using a digital painting app for the first time, with no formal art training, who wants to understand why professional artists paint in grey before adding any colour — and how to do it themselves.

---

### What is the Value & Lighting stage?

Before adding any colour to a painting, experienced artists paint a small version of the whole image using only shades of grey — from near-white through to near-black. This grey rehearsal is not a step you skip to save time; it is the step that makes everything that comes after it easier and more reliable.

Here is why it matters. Imagine a black-and-white photograph of a street at night. Even without any colour, you can tell which parts are lit by the streetlamp and which are in darkness — you can read the scene clearly. Now imagine a poorly lit photo where everything is the same flat medium grey: you cannot tell what is near, what is far, what is important, or where the light is coming from. That flat grey version is what a painting looks like when the lighting has not been worked out. The grey rehearsal — the value study — is how you make sure your painting is the well-lit version, not the flat grey version, before you ever pick up a colour.

---

### Key ideas, without the jargon

**Why grey before colour — the rehearsal pass**

Think of the grey version as a rehearsal for a stage play. In rehearsal, the actors run through the whole performance without costumes or lighting effects — just the words and the blocking. Mistakes are cheap to fix in rehearsal. The value study works the same way: you run through the whole lighting plan without colour. If the scene reads clearly in rehearsal, it will read even more clearly on opening night with the full production. If it does not read in rehearsal, adding costumes and lights will not fix the blocking.

**The bright team and the dark team**

Every surface in your image belongs to one of two teams. The bright team: surfaces that the main light is shining directly onto — the top of a head, the front of a forearm, the nearest face of a box sitting in sunlight. The dark team: surfaces turned away from the light, hidden from it, or sitting in the shadow thrown by another object. The single most important rule for a readable painting is to keep these two teams clearly separated. The moment every patch of the image drifts to the same in-between grey, the image looks flat and you cannot tell what is lit and what is not.

Think of it like the two sides of a building on a sunny afternoon: the side facing the sun is bright, the side facing away is dark. If both sides were painted the same grey, you would not know which way the sun was coming from. Keeping the teams distinct is what tells the viewer where the light is.

**The focal point gets the most contrast**

Your eye always goes to the place in an image where dark and light are most different — the biggest jump from one to the other. You can use this to control exactly where the viewer looks first. The spot you most want them to notice — the face, the glowing lantern, the main character's hand — should have the biggest dark-next-to-light jump anywhere in the image. Every other area gets a smaller jump. Think of a cinema screen showing a film at night: the brightest, most contrasting moment on screen is the one the whole audience watches. Quieter, lower-contrast areas around it let the eye rest between moments of attention.

**Cast shadows anchor things to the ground**

Place a mug on a table and look at where it meets the surface. There is a thin near-black line right at the base, and beyond that a dark patch extending away from the mug in the direction opposite the window. Without those two things — the contact dark and the cast shadow — the mug looks like it is hovering in mid-air. Adding both is the single cheapest way to make an object look like it is genuinely sitting on something solid. The direction of the cast shadow also tells viewers where the light is coming from, so it needs to point in the right direction for your chosen light source.

**When colour looks wrong, check the grey version first**

One of the most disorienting things in digital painting is having colours that feel muddy or "off" and not knowing why. The answer is almost always in the grey version, not the colour itself. If the value structure underneath is flat — if the bright team and dark team have blended into grey porridge — then no colour choice will look clean on top of it. Before adjusting any colour, desaturate the image and look at the grey version. If the grey is flat and hard to read, fix that first. If the grey is solid and clear, the problem actually is the colour.

---

### What to do in your software

1. **Create a new layer and set your palette to grey only.** In most apps (Photoshop, Krita, Photopea, Procreate), you can temporarily set the foreground and background swatches to black and white, or just paint using only desaturated colours. You want no hue at all — just shades of grey from near-black to near-white.

2. **Block in two flat tones first.** Fill every lit surface with a single light grey (around 70–80% brightness) and every shadowed surface with a single dark grey (around 20–30% brightness). Do not blend yet — just decide which team each area belongs to and fill it flat. Squint at the result. Can you read what is lit and what is not?

3. **Place your highest contrast at the focal point.** Find the area you most want the viewer to notice first. Make sure it has a clearly dark area right next to a clearly light area — the biggest jump in the image. Then look at everything else and ask: is any other area competing? Knock it down a little if it is.

4. **Add the contact dark line under every object.** On a new layer, use a very small hard brush at full opacity and near-black to paint a thin line right where each object meets the surface below it. This single step will make objects look grounded rather than floating.

5. **Add the cast shadow.** Extend a darker patch away from the base of each object, in the direction opposite your light source. The shape of this patch can be rough — it just needs to be pointing the right way and clearly darker than the lit ground around it.

6. **Do a thumbnail check.** Zoom out until your image is very small, or hold the canvas at arm's length. Does the focal area still read clearly? Do the bright areas and dark areas form a coherent picture even at that small size? If yes, the value study is ready.

7. **Lock it before adding colour.** Save a copy of the greyscale layer (or merge a flattened copy to a locked layer) before starting Stage 07. Keep it visible while you work with colour so you can compare against it any time something feels off.

---

### Common traps

**Blending grey into grey until everything is the same grey.** This is the most common problem. You start with a good bright team and dark team, then spend time blending and refining, and gradually the two teams drift toward each other until the whole image is a flat medium grey. The fix is to stay aware of team membership: any time you soften an edge between a lit surface and a shadow surface, check that the lit side is still clearly lighter than the shadow side after you have blended. If they are now the same grey, push them apart again using Levels or Curves.

**Adding colour before the grey version works.** This is the equivalent of painting the walls of a room before checking the plaster is dry. The grey version takes maybe 20–40 minutes for a simple composition. Skipping it to "save time" usually results in spending hours trying to fix colour problems that are actually grey problems, which is much slower. The rehearsal pass earns back its time.

**Putting the highest contrast in the wrong place.** If you accidentally make a background element — a bright window, a pale wall, a reflective floor — the highest-contrast thing in the image, the viewer's eye will go there first instead of to your subject. Squint at the study and ask: where does my eye land first? If the answer is not the focal point, find what is pulling attention away and lower its contrast.

**Forgetting that all shadows are one team.** Each shadow area — the shadow on a face, the shadow under a chin, the cast shadow on the floor — should sit within a similar range of dark grey. If one shadow is near-black and another in the same scene is only a mid-grey, the viewer unconsciously reads this as two different light sources, which makes the lighting feel broken. Keep the whole dark team at a similar depth.
