# Stage 10 — Atmosphere & Polish

**Status:** Work in progress.

Stage 10 is the last creative stage before export. Its job is to unify everything that has been built across the previous nine stages: push distant areas back into the hazy distance, confirm that forms are anchored to the ground, and run a short series of checks that reveal problems hiding in plain sight. When this stage is done, the image should hold together at any viewing size, under black-and-white, and after a horizontal flip — and every edge, shadow, and colour shift should point the viewer toward the same focal area.

---

## What You Do at This Stage

- Apply atmospheric (aerial) perspective: reduce contrast, saturation, and edge sharpness on elements that are farther from the viewer, and shift their colour toward the tone of the surrounding air.
- Ensure forms are connected to the ground through cast shadows and contact darkening directly beneath and around them.
- Run the flip check: temporarily flip the canvas horizontally and look for tilts, imbalances, or lopsided compositions that you stopped noticing because you had been staring at the image in one orientation.
- Run the thumbnail check: zoom out to 20–25% (or export a small copy) and verify that the image reads clearly at a tiny size — the focal area should still stand out and the major shapes should still be readable.
- Run the desaturation check: desaturate a flattened copy and confirm that the value structure — light areas light, dark areas dark, focal point clearly the most contrasting spot — still holds with all colour removed.
- Audit edges: the hardest, sharpest edges should sit at the focal area; edges should soften progressively as they move toward the periphery and toward the background.
- Verify light-direction consistency: trace the light across every form and every cast shadow in the image to confirm they all agree on the same single source direction.
- Make final targeted adjustments on non-destructive layers.

---

## Stage Sign-off Condition

All of the following are true:

- Distant elements are clearly lower in contrast and saturation than foreground elements, and shifted in colour toward the air.
- Every object that rests on a surface is connected to that surface by a cast shadow or contact darkening — nothing floats.
- The image still reads clearly at thumbnail size: the focal area dominates and major shapes are identifiable.
- A desaturated copy still shows a clear value hierarchy: the most important area has the strongest value contrast.
- The hardest, highest-contrast edges are at the focal area; edges soften moving outward and into the background.
- All cast shadows and lit surfaces in the image agree on one consistent light direction.
- No structural or hierarchical problems remain that would require changes to earlier stages.

---

## Connections to Other Stages

- **Stage 05 (Perspective & Construction):** Atmospheric perspective is the aerial complement to linear perspective. Both serve depth. If linear construction was well-resolved, atmospheric shifts in Stage 10 reinforce rather than fight that spatial reading.
- **Stage 06 (Value / Lighting):** The desaturation check at this stage verifies that the value structure built in Stage 06 has survived the colour and rendering work done in Stages 07–09. If the check fails, the value structure — not the colour — is the thing to fix.
- **Stage 07 (Colour & Palette):** Atmospheric colour shifts (distant areas cooling and graying toward the air) extend the colour temperature logic set up in Stage 07. The dominant temperature of the atmosphere becomes the tint toward which all distant forms drift.
- **Stage 09 (Rendering & Detail):** The edge audit at Stage 10 confirms that the edge hierarchy established during rendering is holding: focal area hardest, periphery softest.
- **Stage 11 (Critique, Revision & Export):** Stage 10 is the final creative pass. Stage 11 checks the image against the original brief, handles any critique-driven revision, and manages the export.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **All distances look equally sharp and saturated.** Without atmospheric perspective, the background competes with the foreground and the image feels flat, as if everything is on the same layer of cardboard.
- **Objects appear to float.** Forms without cast shadows or contact darkening have no visual connection to the surface below them. They look cut-out rather than placed.
- **Problems only visible when zoomed out are ignored.** Small-scale and thumbnail-scale issues — a value that merges with its neighbour, a shape that loses its identity at a distance — are only visible at reduced size. Skipping the thumbnail check means these survive into the final file.
- **Colour hides a collapsed value structure.** A vivid palette can mask the loss of value contrast until the image is desaturated. Skipping the desaturation check allows this to go undetected.
- **The focal area's edges are no harder than the background.** If every edge in the image is the same softness, the eye cannot find where to land. Hard edges at the focal area act like bold text on a page — they demand attention first.
- **Inconsistent light direction noticed too late.** Cast shadows pointing in different directions, or highlight positions that imply different light sources on different objects, undermine the spatial logic of the whole image. Catching this at Stage 10 is possible; catching it after export is not useful.

## Plain-English Guide

> **Who this is for:** A hobbyist digital artist at an early-to-intermediate level with no formal art training, working in any painting app.

### What is the Atmosphere & Polish stage?

Think about the last time you packed a bag for a trip. You filled it with everything you needed across multiple packing sessions — clothes, charger, toiletries, shoes. Then, before zipping it up, you did a final pass: Is everything in? Is the heavy stuff at the bottom? Did I forget anything obvious? That final check before closing the bag is what Stage 10 is. The painting is essentially done. This stage is about confirming it all holds together, catching anything you stopped seeing because you have been staring at it for hours, and making the small adjustments that turn "mostly finished" into "finished."

---

### Key ideas, without the jargon

**The air itself has a colour, and it fades everything behind it.**

Go to any window on a clear day and look at the view. The things nearest to you — a tree, a wall, a car — are dark and vivid, with clear edges. Things a kilometre away are paler and hazier. Things several kilometres away are nearly the same pale grey-blue as the sky itself. The air is not invisible over long distances: it is slightly blue-grey, and looking through more of it washes out the colour and blurs the edges of anything behind it. In a painting, anything far away should be lighter, less vivid, and softer-edged than anything close. If the background mountains are painted the same rich dark green and sharp-edged as the foreground tree, both feel like they are at the same distance — the air has not been painted.

**Objects need to be attached to the ground.**

Pick up a coffee cup and hold it an inch above a table. It looks wrong immediately, even before your brain has worked out why. The cup is not touching anything — it is suspended. In a painting, any object sitting on a surface needs a dark patch directly where it meets that surface. Even a small darkening where the base of a vase touches the shelf, or where a figure's foot meets the ground, makes the object feel planted. Without it, the object looks like a cut-out photograph glued onto the scene rather than something that actually exists in the space.

**Your eyes stop seeing mistakes they have looked at for too long.**

You have been working on this painting for hours. Your brain has memorised it and is now completing the picture from memory rather than from what is actually on the screen. The horizontal flip is a simple trick to defeat this: mirror the canvas left-to-right and suddenly you are looking at a version of your image you have never seen before. Every tilt you missed, every shape that has crept off-centre, every lopsided weight distribution — all of them become obvious because your brain cannot fill them in from memory. The image is temporarily a stranger, and strangers get looked at fresh. Flip, look, note the problems, then flip back and fix them.

**Zoom out until the detail disappears, and check if the image still works.**

Hold a painting across the room at arm's length. Or zoom out in your app until your image is about the size of a postage stamp. At that size, all the detail you spent hours on vanishes. What remains is just a pattern of light patches and dark patches. Ask: does my focal area — the face, the object, the light source — still stand out from everything else? Is the composition still readable as a clear arrangement? A well-built painting should answer yes to both at thumbnail size. If the focal area disappears into the surroundings when you zoom out, the contrast there needs to be stronger.

**Strip the colour away and check the structure underneath.**

In your app, add a black-and-white adjustment layer on top of everything, or desaturate a flattened copy, and look at the result. With colour gone, ask: does the lightest thing in the image appear at or near the focal area? Does the darkest thing appear nearby or behind the focal area, creating a strong contrast? Does the background recede — is it staying in the mid-tones rather than competing with the foreground? A painting whose value structure works in black-and-white is sound. Colour builds on top of a working structure; it cannot repair a broken one.

**Sharpest edges where you want attention, softer edges everywhere else.**

Imagine a theatre spotlight. The actor standing in the circle of light has a sharp, clear outline — you can see every button on their coat. The actor standing just outside the spotlight, in the half-light, is slightly blurry at the edges. The curtains at the back are even softer, fading into the dark. A painting works on the same principle. The sharpest edge in the image — where two very different tones or colours meet at a crisp line — attracts the eye more powerfully than anywhere else. Make the sharpest edges sit at your focal area. Soften edges as you move away from it. Backgrounds that are deliberately soft do not compete for attention with the subject.

**Every shadow in the painting should agree on who the sun is.**

If you have a figure in a park and the shadows under their chin point left while the shadow of the tree behind them points right, the painting is implying two suns. The viewer's brain detects this immediately, even if they cannot name what is wrong. Before calling the painting done, pick a direction — top left, top right, straight overhead — and trace every single cast shadow and every highlighted surface across the whole image. They should all agree. Fix any that have drifted.

### What to do in your software

1. **Push distant areas back.** Select the background layer or group in your painting. Using a Hue/Saturation adjustment, reduce the Saturation of the background significantly — not to grey, but far enough that it reads as quieter than the foreground. Then use Curves or Levels to pull the darkest values in the background up toward a lighter value, compressing the background's contrast range. Finally, if you have a Gaussian Blur available, apply a very slight blur to the background to soften its edges. Toggle back and forth with and without the adjustments to make sure the background is clearly receding.

2. **Check for floating objects.** On a new layer set to Multiply blend mode at a low opacity, paint a very dark colour directly where each object meets its surface — under a foot, beneath a vase, along the base of a wall. These contact shadows should be darkest right at the contact point and fade out quickly. Even a tiny darkening at the contact zone makes the object feel planted.

3. **Flip the canvas.** In Photoshop or Photopea: Image > Image Rotation > Flip Canvas Horizontal. In Procreate: Canvas > Flip Horizontal. In Krita: Image > Mirror Image > Mirror Horizontally. Look at the flipped version for 30 seconds and note anything that now looks tilted, unbalanced, or wrong. Flip back and fix those things. Flip and check again before continuing.

4. **Run the thumbnail check.** In your app, zoom out to 20–25% (use the zoom slider or Ctrl/Cmd + minus). Squint at the tiny image. If the focal area is still clearly the brightest or highest-contrast spot, and if the main shapes are still readable, the image passes. If the focal area disappears or the main shapes merge into each other, zoom back in and increase the contrast at the focal area or darken what is behind it.

5. **Run the desaturation check.** Flatten a copy of your image (Flatten Image / Merge Visible, then undo immediately if you do not want to lose the layers). Desaturate the copy: Image > Adjustments > Hue/Saturation > Saturation to -100, or add a Black & White adjustment layer. Look at the result. Does the lightest area sit at your focal point? Is the background clearly mid-tone, not competing with the subject? Fix any value problems you find, then discard the desaturated copy.

6. **Audit edges.** Look at the focal area of your painting. Are the transitions there between light and dark areas cleanly sharp — a clear, crisp line? Now look at the edges in the background and at the periphery. Are those transitions softer and more gradual? If the background has edges as hard as the focal area, select those background edges and run a Gaussian Blur or use a soft eraser at low opacity to soften them. The focal area should have the hardest edges in the image.

7. **Check the light direction.** Pick your light source and trace it mentally across every form in the image. The lit side of every object should face the same direction. Every cast shadow should fall on the same side. If you find an object whose shadow goes the wrong way, or whose highlight appears on the wrong side, fix it now. This is often a small adjustment to a highlight or shadow — catching it at this stage costs minutes; missing it means it appears in the final file.

### Common traps

**Thinking atmospheric haze is only for landscape paintings.** Even a character portrait in a simple room benefits from this principle: the objects behind the figure should have less contrast and slightly softer edges than the figure itself. The principle applies anywhere there is depth, even over short distances.

**Adding haze evenly to everything.** Atmospheric haze affects distance, not importance. Do not blur or desaturate the focal area just because it is in the background of someone else's scene. The focal area keeps its contrast and sharpness regardless of depth. Only the non-focal, non-foreground areas get the atmosphere treatment.

**Trusting what you see without the flip.** Many artists skip the flip because the painting "looks fine." The point of the flip is precisely that it shows you what you cannot see when you look at the familiar orientation. Do it every time, even when you are confident — the confidence is exactly the problem the flip is designed to defeat.

**Desaturating and then deciding colour is the issue.** If the desaturated version reveals a value problem, the fix is the value — not more colour. Adding brighter colours on top of a weak grey structure does not fix the grey structure. It just makes it harder to see.

**Making every edge soft in the name of being painterly.** Softening all the edges to create a "painterly" effect is the same as making all the edges hard — it removes hierarchy. Painterly does not mean uniformly soft; it means soft edges where form is turning or receding, and hard edges where contrast is intentional and focal. Keep the hardest edges at the focal area even in a soft, atmospheric style.
