# Pipeline Overview — From Planning to Completion

**Status:** Work in progress.

A professional digital-art pipeline moves an idea from brief to finished, presentable file through a sequence of decisions that progressively reduce uncertainty. Each stage has a governing fundamental; skipping a stage or solving its problems too late is the most common source of rework.

| 09 | Rendering & Detail | Where does the eye rest and where does it travel? | Composition, Light & Value |
| 10 | Atmosphere & Polish | Are depth, focus, and final adjustments coherent? | Perspective (atmospheric), Colour, Light |
| 11 | Critique, Revision & Export | Does the image meet the brief and technical requirements? | All + Critique methods |

## Core Principles Across the Pipeline

1. **Solve large problems early.** Composition, value hierarchy, and major proportions are cheapest to change at the thumbnail and block-in stages.
2. **Separate concerns.** Value can be designed before colour; construction can be solved before surface detail.
3. **One decision at a time.** Each stage should leave a clear, checkable result before the next stage begins.
4. **Primary sources of truth.** The approved thumbnail, the value study, and the construction drawing remain primary references; later stages should not silently override them without conscious revision.

## Plain-English Guide

> **Who this is for:** Someone opening a digital painting app for the first time with no formal art training.

### What is the pipeline?

A pipeline is just a set of steps you do in a specific order so you catch problems early, when they are cheap to fix, instead of late, when they are buried under hours of detailed work. Think of building a flat-pack bookshelf: you lay out all the planks first, check you have every piece, then fit the frame together, then add the shelves, and only at the end do you tighten the final screws and wipe off the pencil marks. If you sand and varnish a plank before checking it fits, you may have to redo that work. A digital painting pipeline works the same way — each step has one job, you check it is done, and only then do you move to the next.

### The key ideas, without the jargon

**"Brief" — your goal written down before you start:** Before touching the canvas, write one or two sentences describing what the finished image should do: who will see it, what mood it should give them, what the single most important thing in it is. Think of it like a shopping list before you go to the supermarket — without it, you wander and forget things. Without a written goal, a painting tends to drift.

**"Ideation" — trying out ideas quickly on paper or in a notes app:** This is the brainstorming phase where you jot down several different directions the image could go — quick written notes, rough word descriptions, or the roughest imaginable doodles — before committing to any one of them. Like a chef thinking through three possible dishes before deciding what to cook: cheap to change your mind at this point, expensive later.

**"Thumbnailing" — coin-sized rough sketches to test the layout:** Before a film director starts filming, they sketch tiny comic-strip panels showing roughly where each actor will stand. The drawings are messy and small — just enough to check whether the scene works as a picture. Thumbnails in a painting are the same thing: a dozen coin-sized rough arrangements drawn fast to find the strongest one. If the layout is dull in a thumbnail the size of a postage stamp, adding detail will not fix it.

**"Silhouette pass" — checking the outer outline tells the right story:** Strip away every internal detail in your mind and just look at the outer edge of your main subject. Imagine cutting the shape out of black paper. Does that black cutout tell you clearly what the subject is — a running figure, a dragon, a spaceship? If the cutout is a vague blob, the pose or shape needs work before any detail is added.

**"Value pass" — painting the light and dark areas in grey before adding any colour:** Squint at any photograph until your eyes are almost closed and the colour disappears — what remains is a pattern of bright blobs and dark blobs. A value pass is a small black-and-white rough version of your painting that tests whether those blobs form a clear, readable picture. If the important shapes read clearly in grey, colour will only make them stronger. If they do not read in grey, no amount of colour will fix them.

**"Colour pass" — adding colour on top of an already-working grey image:** Once the grey version is solid, you add colour in a separate step rather than all at once. Think of grey-scale printing: a well-composed black-and-white photo already looks good before you add any tint. Separating colour from light-and-dark means you can fix either one without disturbing the other.

**"Rendering" — adding light and shadow to make flat shapes look solid:** Look at a poster where every area is one flat, even colour — it looks like a cartoon cutout. Now imagine going back over it with a soft pencil, darkening the sides that face away from a lamp and brightening the sides that face toward it. The flat shapes suddenly look like real, round, touchable objects. That is rendering: applying light-and-shadow information so the painting feels like it exists in three-dimensional space.

**"Locked reference" — an approved earlier decision that later steps must not undo:** Once you have a thumbnail or a grey version you are happy with, save it somewhere visible and treat it as a locked reference — the authoritative version of that decision. If a later step accidentally makes the composition muddier or shifts the values, the locked reference shows you exactly what changed and where to restore it. Like a photograph of a room layout taken before movers arrive: if a chair gets shifted, the photo settles where it goes back.

**"Stage sign-off" — a short check before moving on:** Before moving from any one step to the next, spend two minutes checking that the current step actually works: does the layout make sense? Are the proportions right? Does the grey version read clearly? This is like a pilot running a pre-flight checklist — not because they have forgotten how to fly, but because "I think it looks fine" is not the same as "I have checked it." Catching a proportion problem before the detailed drawing is a one-minute fix; catching it after the rendering is done can mean hours of rework.

### What this means for you step by step

1. **Write your goal (Brief & Ideation).** One or two sentences: what should this image be, who is it for, what feeling should it give? Jot down a few different directions before committing.
2. **Collect real photos to look at (Reference Gathering).** Find photographs of the real things you plan to paint — people, objects, lighting setups — and keep them visible alongside your canvas. You are not copying them; you are using them so your shapes and proportions are grounded in reality rather than guesswork.
3. **Draw coin-sized rough layouts (Thumbnailing & Composition).** Fill a corner of a blank canvas with a dozen tiny, rough arrangements. Keep them rough: just dark and light shapes, no detail. Pick the strongest one before drawing anything at full size.
4. **Check the outer outline (Gesture / Silhouette).** Squint at your chosen thumbnail. Can you read the subject clearly from the cutout shape alone? Fix any vague or stiff outlines before going further.
5. **Build the structure with simple geometric shapes (Perspective & Construction).** Rough in your main objects as boxes and cylinders to check that they sit convincingly on a flat surface and that nothing looks like it is floating or leaning. Fix structure now, before any surface detail exists on top of it.
6. **Paint a grey version to test the lighting plan (Value / Lighting).** Using only black, white, and a few shades of grey, paint a small rough version. Squint at it. If the important parts stand out clearly and the less important parts recede, the lighting plan is solid.
7. **Add colour on top (Colour & Palette).** Now bring in colour, keeping the grey version visible for comparison to make sure the colour choices support rather than fight the dark-and-light plan you already built.
8. **Paint the surfaces — how they catch the light (Form & Materials).** Add the detail of how each surface responds to the light source: smooth surfaces reflect sharply, rough surfaces scatter the light softly.
9. **Focus the detail where the eye should land (Rendering & Detail).** Add fine detail selectively — more in the focal point, less toward the edges. Detail everywhere is the same as detail nowhere.
10. **Final check and tidy (Atmosphere & Polish).** Adjust any colours that feel slightly off, sharpen the focal point, add depth haze if needed, and check the whole image at thumbnail size one final time.
11. **Check it meets your original goal, then export (Critique, Revision & Export).** Go back to the goal you wrote in step 1. Does the image achieve it? Fix anything that does not, then export at the correct file size for where the image will be used.

### Common traps to avoid

**Starting with the detail before the layout is set.** Drawing eyelashes before the face proportions are correct — or painting textures before the light direction is decided — nearly always means that detail has to be repainted. The pipeline is ordered so each step is a stable foundation for the one above it. Skipping ahead feels faster in the moment but costs more time overall.

**Skipping the grey version and going straight to colour.** Colour is distracting: a bold red will look "wrong" even when the underlying light design is the problem, and a muddy grey will look "fine" even when the colour is the problem. Solving light and dark in grey first means you are only solving one thing at a time.

**Treating the thumbnail as a throwaway rather than a locked decision.** If you spend ten minutes finding a strong layout in a thumbnail and then ignore it when you scale up to the full canvas, you lose the work you already did. Keep the approved thumbnail visible and check against it at every stage.

**Adding more detail to fix a structural problem.** If the painting feels flat or unconvincing, the instinct is often to add more texture or more highlight. Usually the real issue is that the grey version was never solid, or the outer shapes were vague to begin with. More detail on a weak foundation makes the foundation harder to fix, not easier.
