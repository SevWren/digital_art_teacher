# Stage 02 — Reference Gathering

**Status:** Work in progress.

Reference gathering is the stage where you collect real visual evidence — photographs, studies, screenshots — before drawing anything. It sits between deciding what you want to make (Stage 01) and working out how to arrange it (Stage 03). The purpose is to replace guesswork with accurate information. A reference board does not tell you what to paint; it tells you what things actually look like, so the choices you make later are grounded in reality rather than a blurry memory of a thing.

---

## What You Do at This Stage

- Identify what categories of information you need: the structure or shape of your main subject, how light behaves on it, what its surface looks like up close, and (if relevant) the environment it sits in.
- Collect photographs or other visual sources covering each category. Aim for at least two or three sources per category so you can cross-check them.
- Break each reference down into what it actually supplies: structural outline, value range, surface texture direction, color temperature of light versus shadow, and so on. Annotate or mentally label each image with what you are using it for.
- Organize the board so references are grouped by what they answer — structure separate from lighting, lighting separate from surface detail.
- Keep the board visible alongside the canvas throughout the rest of the pipeline.

**Governing principle:** Observational accuracy requires good visual data. References are not things to copy; they are the raw material you analyze and extract information from.

---

## Stage Sign-off Condition

You can move on when:

- The reference board exists and covers the four categories relevant to your subject (structure, lighting, surface/material, environment where needed).
- You can state, for each reference, what specific question it answers — not just "it looks cool," but "this shows how the shadow wraps around a curved surface under soft overhead light."
- There are no major visual questions about your subject that remain entirely unanswered by the board.

---

## Common Failure Modes

**Collecting mood instead of information.** Saving images because they feel right or look inspiring, without identifying what specific data they provide. An inspiration board and a reference board are different tools. A reference board answers structural and observational questions; an inspiration board answers tonal and directional ones. Keep them separate.

**Single-source references.** Using one photograph for everything. A single photo locks in one angle, one light direction, one moment in time. Cross-referencing two or three sources lets you distinguish what is true of the subject from what was true of that particular photograph.

**Invisible references.** Collecting references and then not looking at them. The reference board only works if it is visible while you draw. A second monitor, a pinned browser window, or a phone propped beside the screen all work.

**Treating references as things to copy.** Tracing or directly reproducing a reference photograph produces a copy of that photograph, not a painting grounded in understanding. The goal of analysis is to extract the underlying rules — how the light behaves, what the proportions are, how the surface turns — so you can apply those rules freely in your own composition.

**Skipping references for "simple" subjects.** Objects that seem easy to draw from memory — hands, chairs, trees, water — are typically the subjects where memory is most unreliable. The more familiar a subject feels, the more likely you are to draw a symbol for it rather than the thing itself.

## Plain-English Guide

> **Who this is for:** Someone opening a digital painting app for the first time with no formal art training.

---

### What is Reference Gathering?

Before a cook prepares a dish they have never made before, they look at a photograph of how the finished dish should look and read through a recipe that describes how it is built. They are not copying the photograph. They are using it so they know what color, shape, and texture to aim for — so they are working from real information rather than trying to remember what the dish looks like from a visit to a restaurant three years ago.

Reference gathering in digital painting works exactly the same way. Before you start drawing, you find real photographs of the things you plan to paint and keep them visible while you work. Not to trace them. Not to copy them. To use them as a source of accurate information so that your shapes, proportions, and lighting decisions are based on evidence rather than guesswork.

Memory is unreliable for visual detail. Artists at every level — including professionals — use reference constantly. Using reference is not a shortcut or a cheat; it is how accurate work gets made.

---

### The key ideas, without the jargon

**References are visual data, not things to copy.**

Think of a carpenter who is building a chair they have never built before. Before cutting any wood, they look at several photographs of chairs: how the legs attach to the seat, what angle the back sits at, roughly what the proportions are. They are not photocopying the chair in the picture and selling it as their own. They are using the photographs to understand how chairs are built, so their own chair will stand up, look right, and function correctly. Your reference board is that set of photographs: it answers the question "how does this thing actually work?" before you start building.

**There are four kinds of information a reference board needs to cover.**

A complete reference board answers four different questions about your subject:

1. *Shape and structure* — What does the overall outline look like? What are the proportions? Think of this like a blueprint: you need to know how big each part is relative to the others before you start drawing.
2. *Lighting* — Where does the light come from, and how does the shadow fall? How dark is the shadow compared to the lit side? Is there a reflected light bouncing back from below? Think of this like studying how a lamp throws shadows across a room before deciding where to place your furniture.
3. *Surface and material* — Is the surface smooth or rough? Does it catch sharp reflections or scatter the light softly? Think of the difference between photographing a mirror and a piece of damp cardboard: the same light source produces completely different results on those two surfaces.
4. *Environment* — If your subject is sitting inside a scene, what does the world around it look like? What does the ground look like, the sky, the air between things?

You do not always need all four for a simple painting. But knowing which categories you have covered — and which you are guessing — is the difference between controlled choices and happy accidents.

**Analyze the reference; do not just look at it.**

There is a difference between glancing at a photograph and actually extracting information from it. To extract information, ask specific questions: Where is the darkest point in the shadow? Does the edge of the shadow look sharp or blurry? How wide is the lightest stripe compared to the whole shape? What direction do the surface marks or grain run?

Think of a detective studying a crime scene photograph rather than a tourist glancing at a postcard. The tourist sees "a street." The detective notices the specific angle of a shadow, the make of a vehicle, the direction a footprint faces. You want to be the detective — looking at the reference until you can answer specific questions from it, not just until it feels familiar.

**Organize the board so you can find things quickly.**

A pile of unorganized photographs is harder to use than a well-arranged board. Group your references by what they answer: structural photos together, lighting photos together, surface close-ups together. This means that when you are halfway through painting and wondering how the shadow wraps around this part of the form, you can go directly to your lighting references without scrolling through everything.

---

### What to actually do in your software

Most digital painting programs let you open a reference image in a second window alongside your canvas, or pin it to one side of the screen. The exact method depends on your software — in Photoshop and Photopea you can tile windows; in Clip Studio Paint there is a dedicated reference panel; in Procreate you can import a reference and keep it as a floating overlay. A simpler alternative that works in any program: open your references in a browser window and resize it to sit beside your painting canvas, or use your phone as a reference screen.

Steps to follow:

1. Write down the four categories (structure, lighting, surface, environment) and decide which ones you need for your specific subject. A simple still-life may only need structure and lighting. A character in a scene will likely need all four.
2. Search for photographs covering each category you need. Aim for two or three images per category so you can cross-check them. Free image searches, your own photo library, or dedicated reference sites all work.
3. For each image, write a short note — even just a few words — about what specific information it provides. "This one shows how the shadow wraps under the chin in soft overhead light." This forces you to analyze rather than just collect.
4. Arrange them in a visible place alongside your canvas so you are never more than a glance away from the information.
5. Before moving to the next stage, check: have you got something that answers each of the four categories you identified? If there are gaps, fill them now. Gaps filled later, after hours of drawing, are harder to act on.

---

### Common traps to avoid

**Spending an hour collecting references and then closing the window.**
References that are not visible are references you will not use. The whole point is to have them alongside your canvas while you work — not saved in a folder for later consultation that never happens.

**Saving images because they look nice, without knowing what question they answer.**
An inspiring image and a useful reference are different things. An inspiring image makes you want to paint. A useful reference tells you something specific: what the proportions are, how the light falls, what the surface looks like. Both have a place, but only one of them belongs on a working reference board.

**Using a single reference for everything.**
One photograph was taken at one angle, in one kind of light, on one day. If you rely on it exclusively, you will inherit all of its quirks as if they were universal truths. Cross-reference two or three sources so you can see what is consistent across them — that consistency is the reliable information — and what varies between them, which tells you where the subject has flexibility.

**Thinking that needing references means something is wrong.**
Professionals use references for everything: hands, fabric folds, cloud formations, skin tones in specific lights, the way water catches light at a particular time of day. The difference between a beginner and an experienced artist is not that the experienced artist needs fewer references — it is that they know exactly which reference to look for and what to extract from it.
