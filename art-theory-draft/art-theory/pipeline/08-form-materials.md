# Stage 08 — Form & Materials

**Status:** Work in progress.

Stage 08 is where the value structure built in Stage 06 gets translated into convincing surfaces. You are not adding decoration — you are answering the question: does each surface respond to the light in a way that matches what it is made of? Skin behaves differently from metal, metal differently from cloth. At this stage you use the same value system you already have and push it to say something about material as well as form.

---

## What You Do at This Stage

- Model forms by shifting value and temperature according to the established light direction, pulling rounded surfaces through their full progression: bright face → turning surface → core shadow → reflected light.
- Differentiate diffuse surfaces (skin, cloth, unfinished wood) — which scatter light broadly with a soft, wide brightness across the lit face and no sharp glint — from specular surfaces (metal, lacquer, wet skin) — which concentrate light into a small, crisp, high-contrast glint.
- Place highlights at the angle the light source requires: the glint sits exactly where the light bounces directly toward the viewer's eye. Moving it to the wrong place breaks the material reading.
- Add surface texture so it follows the turning of the form — texture marks curve with the surface, they do not lie flat across it like wallpaper. On a curved surface the marks should compress and tilt as they wrap around the edge.
- Maintain the value hierarchy built in Stage 06. Material complexity — subtle highlights, reflected colour, texture variation — lives within the value families already established. No specular glint should be so bright it hijacks the focal hierarchy; no shadow-side texture should be so busy it lightens the shadow group into the light group.

---

## Stage Sign-off Condition

- Each major surface has a clearly differentiated material reading: a viewer should be able to identify broad material categories (hard/shiny, soft/matte, rough/textured) from the painted response to light alone.
- The highlight on every shiny surface sits at the correct angle to the stated light source.
- Surface texture follows the turning of the form — no area of texture reads as a flat decal applied to a surface.
- The value structure from Stage 06 still holds: squinting at the image produces the same bright-team / dark-team separation that the value study established.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Specular glints placed at the centre of the form rather than at the light-source angle.** A highlight in the dead centre of a sphere or an eye looks like a decorative dot rather than a reflection. It must sit where the light direction dictates.
- **All materials painted the same way.** When skin, cloth, and metal all receive the same wide soft brightness, nothing reads as harder or shinier than anything else. Differentiate: shorten and sharpen the bright zone for metal; broaden and soften it for matte cloth.
- **Texture applied as flat pattern.** Pores, fabric weave, or surface grain painted as an even flat tile do not follow the form — they flatten the surface rather than describing it. The marks must tilt and compress as the surface turns away.
- **Material complexity lifts the shadow group.** Adding reflected colour, secondary glints, or texture detail into shadow areas can inadvertently push shadow values up into the mid-tone range, collapsing the separation between light and shadow families. Keep material work within the value families; do not let it blur the boundary.
- **Overblending.** Smooth brush passes that remove all edge variation between material zones produce a rubbery, undifferentiated surface where no material reads convincingly. Some edges between material changes — the rim of a highlight, the edge of a specular streak — should be crisp.

---

## Connections to Other Stages

- **Stage 05 (Perspective & Construction):** The underlying planar structure determines where each surface faces. A surface facing the light source is bright; a surface turned away is dark. Material response lives on top of that structural decision, not instead of it.
- **Stage 06 (Value / Lighting):** The value study is the primary authority for brightness levels at this stage. Material work refines within that structure; it does not override it.
- **Stage 07 (Colour & Palette):** Temperature shifts used in material painting — cooler reflected light into warm shadows, slightly cooler or warmer specular glints depending on light source colour — are governed by the palette locked in Stage 07.
- **Stage 09 (Rendering & Detail):** Stage 08 establishes the material character of large surfaces. Stage 09 then places fine detail selectively at the focal area. Detail density and edge sharpness increase in Stage 09 on top of the material foundation laid here.

## Plain-English Guide

> **Who this is for:** Someone who has done a grey value sketch and added colour, and is now trying to make surfaces look like they are actually made of something — metal, skin, cloth — rather than all the same smooth grey material.

### What is the Form & Materials stage?

Think of painting the side of a house. The painter rolls grey paint over the whole wall to check the coverage before doing anything else. That is the value stage you already finished. Now imagine going back with a second roller — the same wall colour, but this time you are deliberately pressing harder on the patches that face the sun and leaving the shaded areas as they are. And then you take a small brush and run a thin streak of almost-white paint where the top brick ledge catches the harshest direct sunlight. You are not inventing new light; you are describing how each surface responds to the light that is already there.

That is this stage. The light direction is locked. Your job is to paint what happens when that light hits different materials: a rough brick wall scatters it softly, a polished metal door handle concentrates it into a tiny streak, a cotton curtain spreads it gently across its whole face.

### Key ideas, without the jargon

**Matte surfaces spread light like a lampshade — shiny surfaces concentrate it like a mirror.**

Hold a sheet of plain white printer paper under a desk lamp. The whole sheet glows gently, and the brightness spreads evenly across most of its face without any sharp bright dot. Now hold up a spoon under the same lamp. You can see a small, intense reflection of the lamp bulb itself in the bowl of the spoon. The paper is scattering the light in every direction at once — no matter where you stand, the paper looks roughly the same brightness. The spoon is bouncing the light in one specific direction — you have to be standing in almost exactly the right spot to see the bulb's reflection.

Every material sits somewhere on this line between the paper and the spoon. Skin is closer to the paper end. Polished armour is closer to the spoon end. Worn leather is somewhere in the middle. When you paint the lit side of a matte material, you paint a wide, soft area of brightness with no sharp dot. When you paint a shiny material, you paint a smaller lit area and then add one small, very bright, crisp glint at the exact spot where the lamp would bounce toward your eye.

**The glint goes where the light tells it to — not in the middle.**

The small bright spot on a shiny surface — the thing you see on a grape, a wet eye, a polished helmet — is not decoration. It is the reflection of the light source itself. It sits exactly where the light source bounces directly toward the viewer's eye. If your light is coming from the upper left, the glint belongs on the upper left part of the shiny surface. It never sits in the dead centre of an eye or the dead centre of a sphere unless the light is directly in front of and above the viewer. Move the glint to match your light direction and the material reading immediately snaps into place.

**Texture goes with the form — it does not sit on top of it.**

Imagine the cross-sections of an orange. The segments curve over the surface from top to bottom, bending around the rounded form and disappearing around the back. They do not run in straight horizontal lines across the front face like a label on a jar. Surface texture works the same way. Pores on a cheek, threads in fabric, grain in wood — all of these follow the curve of the surface beneath them. As the surface turns away from you, the texture marks tilt and compress. A patch of texture that runs in perfectly parallel lines across a rounded surface looks like a sticker applied over a photograph, not like a real material. Let the marks curve with the form.

**Do not let material detail push shadows into the light.**

Once you add reflected light, coloured bounce, and texture variation into the shadow areas, it is easy for those shadow areas to drift lighter and lighter until they are nearly as bright as the lit side. At that point the image looks flat — the clear separation between the bright team and the dark team that made the value sketch work has been eroded. Keep checking: squint at your painting. If squinting no longer shows you a clear bright zone and a clear dark zone, the material work has moved shadow values up too far. Darken them back and keep the detail subtler.

### What to do in your software

1. **Work on a layer above your colour base layer.** Do not paint material changes directly into your base colour. Add a new layer set to Normal or Multiply (for darkening passes) or Screen/Add (for brightening passes), and paint material cues there. This lets you reduce the opacity of the whole layer if the effect gets too strong.

2. **Decide the material type for each major surface before you paint it.** Is this surface matte, semi-shiny, or highly reflective? Write a quick note or use a layer name. Matte: wide, soft bright zone, no sharp glint. Semi-shiny (leather, damp fabric, skin): moderate bright zone, possible soft smeared highlight. Highly reflective (polished metal, glass, lacquered surface): small bright zone, crisp narrow glint, strong dark-to-light contrast.

3. **For shiny surfaces, find the glint position before you paint it.** Your light source has a direction — if it is upper left, the glint goes on the upper-left-facing part of the surface. Use a very small, hard brush at high opacity to place a single crisp bright stroke or dot at that position. It should be noticeably brighter than the rest of the lit area and have a fairly hard edge. For very rough or semi-shiny materials, use a larger, softer brush at lower opacity instead.

4. **For texture, use strokes that follow the form.** If the surface is curved, set your brush strokes to follow that curve. On a cylindrical arm, horizontal brush strokes that arc slightly with the surface read as going around the arm. Perfectly straight horizontal strokes read as a flat pattern applied to the arm. The easiest check: zoom out and squint. If the texture makes the surface look flatter, the marks are running against the form. If the texture makes it look rounder, the marks are following the form.

5. **Squint-check after every major material area.** After adding glints, texture, and reflected-light variations to one surface, zoom out to full canvas and squint. The bright team and dark team separation should still hold. If the shadow areas on the surface you just worked on are now reading as nearly as bright as the lit areas elsewhere, reduce the opacity of your material layer or darken the shadow-region strokes.

### Common traps

**Painting all materials the same way.** If every surface in your image — clothing, skin, hair, eyes — receives the same soft round brightness with the same soft edge, nothing will look like any specific material. The most useful single change you can make is to vary the size of the lit zone: wide for matte, narrow for shiny. That one difference does more work than almost anything else.

**Putting the glint in the wrong place.** A highlight dot in the centre of an eye or the centre of a forehead is the most common single error at this stage. The glint position tells the viewer where the light source is. If the glint contradicts the rest of the lighting (which you spent Stage 06 setting up), the surface will look wrong and the viewer will not be able to say why. Always check: is this glint on the side that faces the light?

**Texture that flattens the surface.** If you add a fine texture pass and the surface looks flatter afterward, the marks are fighting the form. Either the marks are too uniform, too straight, or too evenly spread across the surface. Try reducing the texture to only the lit area and the soft edge of the bright zone, leaving the shadow area relatively texture-free — the eye will read the whole surface as textured even if you only see it clearly on the lit side.

**Overworking the shadow areas.** The darkest parts of your image are easy to leave alone, but at this stage there is a temptation to add reflected colour, bounce light, and texture variation into the shadows to make them "interesting." Keep these additions quiet — slightly cooler, slightly lighter than the core shadow, but never approaching the brightness of the lit side. The shadow's job is to stay dark; its secondary job is to hold a little warmth or bounce. Its job is not to compete with the light.
