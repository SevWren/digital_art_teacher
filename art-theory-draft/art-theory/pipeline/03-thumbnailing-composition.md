# Stage 03 — Thumbnailing & Composition

**Status:** Work in progress.

At this stage you decide where everything goes in the image — and you make that decision at the smallest, cheapest possible scale before any detailed drawing exists. A thumbnail is a coin-sized rough sketch. You make several of them quickly, pick the strongest arrangement, and lock it as the reference every later stage must agree with. Solving composition here costs minutes; solving it after detailed rendering costs hours.

---

## What You Do at This Stage

- Fill a small area of your canvas with multiple coin-sized rough sketches, each testing a different crop, placement, or arrangement of the main shapes.
- Keep every thumbnail rough: flat dark blobs and flat light blobs only. No line detail, no colour.
- Test the dark-and-light skeleton (notan) at thumbnail size — if the arrangement of dark patches and bright patches does not make a readable picture when squinted at, it will not become readable by adding detail later.
- Check that the outer outline of your main subject reads clearly as a flat cutout shape, without internal detail needed.
- Decide on crop and framing — where the four edges of the canvas fall is part of the composition, not an afterthought.
- Select the single strongest thumbnail, lock it, and keep it visible for every stage that follows.
- Produce a small value sketch from the chosen thumbnail — a slightly larger rough showing the intended dark/light architecture — before moving to full-size construction.

---

## Stage Sign-off Condition

One thumbnail has been selected. A small value sketch exists showing the intended dark-and-light arrangement. Crop and major mass placement are locked. You can state where the viewer should look first and why the chosen arrangement supports that.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Skipping thumbnails and drawing at full size immediately.** Composition problems are buried under hours of detail work before they are discovered. A weak layout does not become strong by being drawn larger.
- **Thumbnails that are too detailed.** Adding hair, facial features, or texture at thumbnail size defeats the purpose. The thumbnail should be readable only as dark and light blobs — nothing finer.
- **Only making one or two thumbnails.** One thumbnail is not an exploration; it is a first instinct drawn slightly smaller. Aim for at least six to eight distinct arrangements before choosing.
- **Choosing the thumbnail you drew first, not the strongest one.** The first idea is rarely the best arrangement. Evaluate thumbnails against each other, not against the blank canvas.
- **Multiple competing centres of equal contrast.** When two or more areas in the thumbnail have the same level of darkness-against-light, the eye has nowhere to land. One area must be clearly the most contrasting.
- **Dark and light patches scattered evenly.** Dozens of small dark specks scattered across the canvas read as noise from a distance. Group the darks into two or three connected patches before evaluating the thumbnail.
- **Ignoring the locked thumbnail at later stages.** If the composition was strong in the thumbnail and the finished painting feels off, the thumbnail was likely abandoned silently. Keep it visible and check against it throughout.

## Plain-English Guide

> **Who this is for:** Someone using Procreate, Krita, Photopea, or Photoshop with no formal art training who wants to know what thumbnailing is, why it matters, and how to do it.

---

### What is thumbnailing?

Before a film director starts shooting a scene, they often sketch a series of tiny comic-strip panels — rough rectangles showing where each actor stands and roughly how the frame is cropped. The drawings are messy and small, sometimes drawn with a single felt-tip pen. They are not the movie. They are cheap tests to find out which version of the scene works before expensive crew and equipment are committed.

Thumbnailing in a digital painting works exactly the same way. You make a set of very small, very rough sketches — each one about the size of a coin — to try out different arrangements of the main shapes in your scene. You keep them rough on purpose. You make several. You pick the strongest one. Only then do you begin drawing anything at full size.

The reason this matters: if you draw a full-size picture and the layout is dull or confusing, fixing it means undoing hours of work. If you test six layouts as coin-sized rough sketches and one of them is dull, you spent thirty seconds on it. The work is cheap enough to throw away without regret.

---

### Key ideas, without the jargon

**The dark-and-light skeleton (notan — that means reducing your image to two or three flat blobs to test the big picture before adding any detail)**

Squint hard at any photograph until the detail blurs away. What you are left with is a handful of dark patches and bright patches. If those patches make an interesting arrangement even when everything is blurred beyond recognition, the image has a strong skeleton. If the patches all run together into a flat medium grey, no amount of added detail will save it.

At the thumbnail stage you are testing only those dark-and-light patches. A thumbnail is nothing but flat dark blobs and flat bright blobs. If the blobs make a clear, interesting picture, you have a strong foundation. If they are scattered evenly everywhere like static on a TV screen, rearrange them before you draw a single line at full size.

Think of it like a lit window at night viewed from outside: dark sky above, dark ground below, one bright rectangle of warm light in the middle. Three big patches — two dark, one bright — and you can read it instantly from across the street. That is the quality you are looking for in a thumbnail.

**Recognisable outline (silhouette readability — that means whether your main subject reads clearly as a distinct shape with all internal detail removed)**

Think of the Batman logo: a black bat shape on a yellow background. Cover the yellow and just look at the black shape. You can tell it is a bat immediately, even though it is a flat blob with no face, no texture, no detail at all. That is a strong outline.

Now imagine a character whose raised sword arm sits at the same angle as their body — the arm and the body merge into one indistinct blob and you cannot count the limbs. That is a weak outline.

At thumbnail size, temporarily paint your main subject as a solid black blob and ask: can I tell what it is? If the blob is a vague lump, the pose or arrangement needs work before any detail is added.

**Deciding what the frame cuts off (cropping — that means where you position the four edges of your canvas)**

Hold your two hands up to make a rectangular viewfinder and look through it at any room. Move your hands closer to your face and the frame grows — more of the room fits in. Move them farther away and the frame tightens. Tilt the frame so a window is cut in half by the right edge — the partial window looks intentional and dynamic; it implies there is more beyond the border.

The four edges of your canvas are part of the composition. Where you cut the scene changes what feels implied, what the eye finds first, and whether the subject feels cramped or has breathing room. Testing different crops is one of the main reasons to make several thumbnails rather than one.

**What the viewer should look at first (hierarchy — that means the order things grab attention)**

Look at any newspaper front page. The biggest, boldest headline is what your eye lands on first. The photo is second. The smaller article text is third. Nobody decided to read it in that order — the sizes and boldness made the decision for them.

In your thumbnail, one area should have the strongest contrast between dark and light. That area is where the viewer's eye will land first, automatically, without being asked. You get to decide which area that is — but you must decide, and you must make it clearly the most contrasting spot. If two or three areas compete equally, the eye wanders and never settles.

**Grouping darks together (value massing — that means keeping dark patches in a few large connected blobs, not scattered everywhere)**

Imagine someone punched fifty tiny holes in a wall and light leaked through each hole randomly. The same amount of light as one window, but chaotic and hard to read. Now imagine one large window: clear, instant, readable from across the street.

Scattered small dark patches across a canvas behave the same way — they create visual noise that is exhausting to read from a distance. Gathering darks into two or three connected patches makes the image readable at any size. This is the test your thumbnail is designed to run: are the darks gathered, or are they scattered?

---

### What to do in your software

1. **Create a small test area.** Either open a new canvas at 200 × 200 pixels, or dedicate a corner of a large canvas. Use a hard round brush. Set your colours to black, white, and one mid-grey.

2. **Make at least six thumbnails.** Each one should take no more than one to two minutes. Paint only flat dark blobs and flat bright blobs — no lines, no detail, no colour. Each thumbnail tests a different arrangement: subject on the left, subject on the right, wide crop, tight crop, subject large, subject small.

3. **Squint at each thumbnail.** Hold your screen at arm's length and squint until the thumbnails blur slightly. The dark patches and bright patches should make a clear, interesting picture even at that blurred size. Any thumbnail where everything blurs into a flat medium grey is a weak arrangement.

4. **Check the outline of your main subject.** On a thumbnail you like, paint over the main subject with solid black. Zoom out. Does the black blob tell you what the subject is? If the pose is unclear as a flat cutout, consider changing the angle or separating the parts so the outline reads on its own.

5. **Pick the strongest thumbnail.** Not the one you drew first. Not the most familiar. The one where the dark-and-light blobs make the most interesting arrangement and the main subject has the most readable outline.

6. **Lock it as your reference.** Screenshot it, duplicate the layer, or set it aside somewhere visible. This thumbnail is now the authority for every stage that follows. Later stages refine it — they do not replace it without a conscious decision to return to this stage.

7. **Make a slightly larger value sketch.** Using the locked thumbnail as your guide, make a version roughly 400–600 pixels wide using only dark, mid-grey, and light. This is the dark-and-light plan for your full painting. It should take five to ten minutes and contain no detail — only the intended arrangement of light and shadow areas.

---

### Common traps

**Drawing at full size before testing layouts.** It feels faster. It is not. One hour of full-size drawing on a weak layout is followed by the realisation that the layout needs to change — and an hour of undoing. Six coin-sized thumbnails take ten minutes and cost nothing to throw away.

**Making thumbnails too detailed.** The moment you start adding hair strands, facial features, or texture at thumbnail size, you have stopped testing the layout and started investing in a specific version. Keep thumbnails rough enough that you could make six of them in ten minutes. If a thumbnail takes longer than two minutes, it is too detailed.

**Only testing one crop.** The first framing that comes to mind is usually the most obvious one — subject centred, full body visible, nothing cut off. Try the uncomfortable version: crop tighter, cut the subject at the waist, put the subject in one corner, try a very wide shot. One of the uncomfortable versions is often the strongest.

**Ignoring the thumbnail once the full-size drawing begins.** The thumbnail represents a decision that took ten minutes of focused comparison. Ignoring it at full size means losing that work silently. Keep the approved thumbnail visible in a corner of your screen and check against it at the end of every session. If the full-size painting has drifted away from the thumbnail's arrangement, decide consciously whether that drift is an improvement or a problem.
