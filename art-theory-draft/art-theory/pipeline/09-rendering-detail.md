# Stage 09 — Rendering & Detail

**Status:** Work in progress.

Rendering and detail is the stage where you decide *where* the eye should rest and *where* it should travel. The core principle is that detail is a tool for directing attention, not a coat of paint applied everywhere. The focal point of the image receives the most detail, the sharpest edges, and the highest local contrast; every other area is progressively quieter. A painting where every region receives equal amounts of detail loses its hierarchy — the eye has nowhere to land and wanders.

This stage depends directly on the value structure established in Stage 06 and the colour and material decisions made in Stages 07–08. It must not silently undo them.

---

## What You Do at This Stage

- Identify the single most important area of the image (the focal point) and confirm it already holds the strongest contrast or brightness from the value pass.
- Add detail selectively: most in the focal area, less in mid-ground elements, least in edges and background.
- Set edge quality throughout the image: hard, crisp edges at and near the focal area; progressively softer or lost edges moving outward into secondary and background regions.
- Add surface texture and micro-detail only where the form warrants it — vary the direction, width, and opacity of marks so they follow the turning of the surface rather than lying flat across it.
- Check stylization consistency: every area of the image should reach a comparable level of finish. A highly rendered face next to a barely-sketched hand is a unity problem, not a time-saving measure.
- Periodically zoom out to thumbnail size (or step back from the screen) and confirm the focal point still reads clearly. If you cannot identify the focal area at thumbnail size, detail has been spread too evenly.

---

## Stage Sign-off Condition

The image reads correctly at thumbnail size: the focal point is immediately identifiable as the area of highest contrast, sharpest edges, and greatest surface detail. Secondary areas support the focal area without competing with it. The level of finish is consistent across all parts of the image. The underlying value structure established in Stage 06 is intact and has not been eroded by detail work.

---

## Common Failure Modes

| What you notice | Likely cause | What to try |
|---|---|---|
| Eye wanders with no clear place to land | Detail distributed evenly across the whole image — no hierarchy | Reduce edge sharpness, contrast, and surface texture in every area except the focal point; let the focal area be the one clearly sharpest and most detailed spot |
| Textures look stuck on like decals | Surface marks are laid flat rather than following the form's direction | Remap marks so their direction, width, value, and edge quality change as the surface curves away from the viewer |
| One area looks heavily finished, another looks barely started | Inconsistent level of finish (stylization inconsistency) | Bring the weaker areas up — add edge definition and surface character so they reach a comparable level of resolution to the finished areas |
| Image looks fine zoomed in but loses all hierarchy at thumbnail size | Local detail decisions have overridden the large-scale value structure | Desaturate and squint — if the focal point no longer reads as the dominant value mass, reduce detail and contrast in competing regions |
| Background feels busy and competes with the subject | Edges and detail in the background are as sharp and dense as in the focal area | Soften background edges and reduce texture density; background areas should whisper, not compete |

## Connections to Other Stages

- **Stage 03 (Thumbnailing & Composition):** The focal hierarchy decided in the thumbnail is the authority for where detail should and should not go. Rendering should reinforce that decision, not override it.
- **Stage 06 (Value / Lighting):** The value structure is the skeleton on which all detail sits. Detail work must not muddy or flatten the value hierarchy. Desaturating and squinting at intervals is the check.
- **Stage 08 (Form & Materials):** Surface material decisions — how rough or smooth a surface is, how it scatters or reflects light — directly determine what kind of marks and edges belong in this stage. Hard glints on smooth metal; broad soft marks on rough cloth.
- **Stage 10 (Atmosphere & Polish):** After detail is placed, atmospheric haze and final global adjustments will further reduce the prominence of distant and secondary areas. Do not over-detail areas that Stage 10 will subsequently quiet.

## Plain-English Guide

> **Who this is for:** A hobbyist digital artist, recreational, early-to-intermediate level, no formal art training.

### What is the Rendering & Detail stage?

You have already set up the light and dark structure, chosen your colours, and painted the broad surfaces. Now you are doing the fine work: adding the sharp edges, the texture of skin or fabric or metal, the small crisp highlights, the subtle marks that make the painting feel real and finished. But here is the trap almost everyone falls into — they add all that fine work *everywhere*, treating the whole canvas like a wall that needs an even coat of paint. When every part of the painting is equally detailed and equally sharp, the eye has no reason to land anywhere in particular. It just wanders. Detail is not decoration. It is a signpost that says: "look here."

---

### Key ideas, without the jargon

**The focal point gets the most detail — everywhere else gets less.**

Think of a stage with a single spotlight. The performer in the beam is crisp, vivid, every button on their jacket visible. The stagehands at the edge of the stage are dimmer, softer, less distinct. You are not ignoring the stagehands — they are still there, still making sense — but your eye goes to the performer without you deciding to look there. The spotlight made the decision. In a painting, you build that spotlight effect by making one area sharper and more detailed than everything else, and letting the surrounding areas quietly step back.

**Hard edges pull the eye in; soft edges push things back.**

Run your fingernail along the surface of a photograph. At the sharp edge where a face meets a dark background, you can trace an exact line. At the edge of a cheek curving away from a lamp, you cannot trace a line at all — the bright side just gradually melts into the darker side. The sharp, traceable edge is called a hard edge. The gradual fade is a soft edge. Hard edges say "important, look here." Soft edges say "secondary, keep moving." Place your hard edges at the focal point. Let edges soften progressively as you move outward toward the corners.

**"Detail everywhere is the same as detail nowhere."**

Imagine a Where's Waldo page: hundreds of equally busy, equally detailed figures all competing for your attention. You cannot immediately see who you are supposed to look at. Now imagine the same page but every figure except Waldo is drawn lightly and simply, and Waldo alone is drawn in full colour with sharp, clear edges. Your eye goes straight to him. The detail on Waldo only works because everything else is simpler. This is why deliberately leaving secondary areas quieter is not laziness — it is what makes your focal area feel important.

**Make your texture marks follow the shape of the surface — not wallpaper the surface.**

Look at the weave on a basketball. On the part of the ball facing you, the lines run in all different directions as the surface curves away in every direction. If you painted those lines as a flat grid going straight across the whole ball, it would look like a flat piece of paper with a grid drawn on it, not a round ball. The marks need to curve and narrow and fade as the surface turns away from you. The same rule applies to any texture — skin, fabric, stone, bark. If the marks all point the same direction regardless of which way the surface is facing, the texture looks like a sticker stuck on top rather than part of the surface.

**All areas of the image should reach a comparable level of finish.**

Imagine a portrait where the face is immaculately rendered — every pore, every eyelash — and then the hand resting on the table is a rough blocky sketch. The hand looks unfinished, not stylised. The face and the hand are living in two different paintings. You do not need to give the hand the same level of microscopic detail as the face, but you do need to bring it to the same *quality* of finish: clean edges, clearly described form, surfaces that respond to the light. The difference between the focal area and the secondary areas should be about how much detail you added, not about whether one area looks done and the other looks abandoned.

**Stop when the image reads correctly at thumbnail size.**

Zoom out until your whole painting is the size of a postage stamp, or hold your phone at arm's length. At that size, can you immediately see which area is the focal point? Does it pop clearly? If yes, you are done adding detail — anything more will only crowd the reading. If the focal point is not immediately obvious at that size, you have either spread detail too evenly, or the focal area needs a slightly stronger contrast or sharper edge to separate it from the rest.

### What to do in your software

1. **Identify your focal point and put a mental fence around it.** Before adding any fine detail, decide: what is the single most important area? The face? The hands? The light source? That is the only area that gets your hardest edges and densest detail. Everything else gets progressively less.

2. **Add detail to the focal area first, on a new layer above your existing work.** Use a hard-edged brush at full opacity for crisp edges and sharp texture marks in the focal area. Keep the brush small. Work inward from the edge of the focal area — the outermost edge of the focal zone is where your hardest edges live; the interior can have both hard and soft marks.

3. **Soften edges as you move outward.** In most apps (Procreate, Photoshop, Krita, Photopea), switch to a soft round brush at low opacity (10–20%) and run it along the edges in secondary areas to blur them gently. The goal is not to erase the edges but to make them untraceably gradual — you can still tell where one area ends and another begins, but you cannot trace a crisp line between them.

4. **Add texture marks that follow the form.** For any surface with visible texture — fabric, skin, rough stone — use short brush strokes that point in the direction the surface is facing at that spot. On a rounded surface like an arm or a ball, the strokes near the edge of the form should curve around the edge, not run straight across it. Vary the opacity of strokes as the surface turns into shadow: marks in the shadow zone should be lower opacity and less distinct than marks in the lit zone.

5. **Check by zooming to thumbnail size after every major addition.** In Procreate: pinch out. In Photoshop or Photopea: Ctrl/Cmd + minus until the whole image is small. In Krita: the Navigator panel. Ask yourself: can I immediately point to the focal area? If not, find the competing area and soften its edges or reduce its detail.

6. **Check stylization consistency before calling the stage done.** Look at each major area of the image in turn — background, secondary figures or objects, foreground elements that are not the focal point. Ask: does each area look like it belongs to the same painting? If one area looks clearly more finished than the others, bring the weaker areas up by sharpening their defining edges and adding basic surface character to match the overall register of the image.

---

### Common traps

**Adding more detail to fix a problem that is not a detail problem.** If the painting feels flat or unconvincing, the instinct is to add more texture, more highlights, more surface marks. Usually the real issue is that the value structure from Stage 06 was never solid — the lit areas and shadow areas are too close in brightness, or the focal area is not actually the most contrasting spot. Adding detail on top of a weak value structure buries the structure further and makes it harder to fix. Desaturate the image and squint before adding anything. If the problem is visible in the grey version, fix it there first.

**Making every edge in the painting the same sharpness.** If you paint with a hard brush throughout — or blend everything with a soft brush throughout — the whole image will feel like it is at the same depth and the same distance from the viewer. The focal area and the background will feel equally close. Vary your edge quality deliberately: hard at the focal point, soft or dissolved in the background and peripheral areas.

**Detailing the background before the focal area is finished.** It is tempting to work on the interesting background detail while the focal area is still rough. But every mark you put in the background becomes a visual competitor for the focal area. Finish the focal area first, establish its contrast and edge quality, and then add background detail only up to the level where it no longer competes.

**Texture that does not turn with the form.** If your texture marks all run in the same direction regardless of what the surface is doing — a flat horizontal grid across a sphere, for example — the surface will read as flat. Take a moment before adding texture to an area and ask: which way is this surface facing right now? Let the marks follow that direction, and narrow or fade them as the surface turns away from you.
