# Stage 01 — Brief & Ideation

**Status:** Work in progress.

Stage 01 is the only stage where you make decisions about what the image must achieve before any mark is made. It combines two closely related tasks: writing a short brief that records the goal and constraints, and generating several rough directions through ideation before committing to one. The governing fundamental is composition — because the brief governs the direction, and the direction determines whether a composition will serve the image's stated purpose. A brief that is weak or absent is the most common single cause of rework across the entire pipeline, because every later stage is evaluated against it.

---

## What You Do at This Stage

### 1. Write the brief

Before opening a new canvas, record the following decisions in writing. A few sentences or a short table is enough. The act of writing forces clarity that "I have a rough idea" does not provide.

| Decision | Options / notes | Effect on later work |
|---|---|---|
| Style register | Photorealistic / detailed realism / stylized / graphic / abstract | Controls edge treatment, detail density, and colour simplification |
| Lighting idea | Single key + fill / soft diffuse / dramatic / coloured lights / multiple sources | Sets value structure and colour-temperature plan |
| Viewpoint | Eye-level / high / low / strong foreshortening / flat | Determines perspective complexity at Stage 05 |
| Background treatment | None / simple tone / suggestive / fully realised | Affects atmospheric and edge work at Stages 09–10 |
| Output goal | Screen only / print / both | Decides colour space and resolution floor |
| Working size | Final pixel dimensions; if print is possible, PPI target | Set canvas to final size from the start — upscaling loses quality |
| Colour space and bit depth | sRGB for screen-only; wider space if print is likely; 16-bit while working if practical | Protects colour range and reduces banding |

**You can move on from brief-writing when:** The brief exists in writing — even a rough paragraph with the table above filled in — and the canvas is created at the correct size and colour settings.

### 2. Ideation: generate multiple directions before committing

Ideation means generating several distinct rough directions that differ in arrangement, lighting mood, emotional tone, or overall feeling — before committing to any one of them. The goal is to evaluate options cheaply. At this stage, directions can be word descriptions, quick written notes, or the roughest imaginable sketches; they do not need to look like finished artwork.

- Aim for at least two or three meaningfully different directions, not slight variations on a single idea.
- Evaluate each direction against the brief: does this arrangement serve the purpose stated in the brief?
- Select one primary direction. Keep the others available — they may become useful if the selected direction stalls later.

Ideation is governed by gesture, intent, emotional tone, and structural readability. A direction chosen at this stage shapes every composition decision in Stage 03.

### 3. Set up the canvas

Once the brief is written and a direction selected, create the canvas:

- Set pixel dimensions to your target output size. Do not plan to upscale later.
- Set colour space: sRGB for screen-only delivery; Adobe RGB or a wider space if print is possible.
- Set bit depth: 16-bit while painting reduces banding and gives headroom for adjustments; convert to 8-bit only for export formats that require it.
- Name and save the file immediately with a clear, date-stamped filename.

---

## Stage Sign-off Condition

You can move on from Stage 01 when:

- The brief exists in writing and all decisions in the table above are recorded.
- One direction has been selected from ideation and you can state, in one sentence, why it fits the brief.
- The canvas is created at the correct size, colour space, and bit depth.

## Common Failure Modes

**Starting without a written brief.** A mental note that "I want to paint a dragon in a forest" is not a brief. It records no decisions about style, light, viewpoint, or output goal. Every ambiguous decision made later will need to be undone or revisited because there is no reference to check against.

**Committing to the first idea without generating alternatives.** Ideation exists because the first direction is rarely the strongest one. Spending three minutes on two additional written directions costs almost nothing and frequently produces a better option than the one that came to mind first.

**Choosing a style register without understanding its downstream cost.** Photorealism requires edge treatment, material response, and lighting at a level of resolution that stylized or graphic approaches do not. Choosing photorealism as a starting point and then drifting toward a looser style mid-pipeline wastes the construction and rendering work that photorealism demands.

**Setting the wrong canvas size and colour space.** Upscaling a low-resolution canvas after significant rendering work has been done does not recover detail. Setting sRGB when the output goal is print means colour information is permanently lost. These decisions are cheapest to make correctly now and most expensive to correct later.

**Treating the brief as a formality rather than a reference.** A brief that is written and then ignored provides no benefit. Its function is to serve as the authority when any later decision feels uncertain. If Stage 07 colour choices feel directionless, or Stage 09 rendering priorities feel unclear, the brief is what you return to.

## Connections to Other Stages

- **Stage 02 (Reference Gathering):** The brief's style register and lighting idea define what kinds of references are needed. A brief that specifies dramatic single-source lighting determines what lighting reference to collect.
- **Stage 03 (Thumbnailing & Composition):** The selected ideation direction goes directly into thumbnailing. Stage 03 tests whether the chosen direction produces a strong composition. If thumbnailing reveals the direction is compositionally weak, it is correct to return to Stage 01 ideation — this is not failure, it is the pipeline working as designed.
- **Stage 06 (Value / Lighting):** The lighting idea locked in the brief is the authority for the value structure. Changing the lighting idea after Stage 03 requires revising the composition.
- **Stage 11 (Critique, Revision & Export):** The final-stage check asks whether the image meets the brief. An absent or vague brief makes the Stage 11 check impossible to perform honestly.

## Plain-English Guide

> **Who this is for:** Someone opening a digital painting app for the first time with no formal art training.

### What is the Brief & Ideation stage?

Before you open a new canvas and start painting, this stage asks you to stop and write down — in plain sentences — what you are trying to make and why. Then it asks you to come up with a few different rough ideas for how to make it, compare them, and pick the strongest one before committing. Think of it like writing a shopping list before you go to the supermarket. Without the list, you wander the aisles, forget things, and come back with items you did not need. With the list, every decision in the shop is quick because you know what you came for. The brief is that list. The ideation is the part where, before leaving the house, you think through whether you really need all those items — or whether a simpler meal would be better.

### The key ideas, without the jargon

**The brief — your goal written down before you start:**
Think of a builder who receives a short written description from a client before drawing a single line: "a two-bedroom house, single storey, with a south-facing garden." That brief governs every decision that follows — the floor plan, the window sizes, the roof pitch. Your brief for a painting works exactly the same way. Write one or two sentences: what should this image show, what mood should it have, where will it be seen (on screen, printed out, posted on social media)? Those sentences become the thing you check back against whenever a decision later in the painting feels unclear.

**Style register — deciding how realistic or simplified the painting will be:**
Look at two pictures of a cat: a photograph, and a simple cartoon with two dots for eyes and a triangle for a nose. Both are cats, but one has every hair, whisker, and shadow; the other is three shapes. "Style register" is just the word for where on that scale your painting sits. Decide this at the start, because a painting that tries to be a photograph in some areas and a cartoon in others tends to look confused. You do not need to know art terms to decide — you just need to look at examples and say: "I want mine to look more like that one."

**Lighting idea — deciding where the light comes from before you draw anything:**
Before a photographer takes a portrait, they decide where to put the light: from one side for dramatic shadow, from above for a neutral look, from behind for a glowing outline. That decision shapes every shadow and every bright patch in the image. In a painting you make the same decision now, in words, before drawing anything: "light from the upper left, warm and strong, with a soft cooler fill from the right." Write it in your brief. Every shadow and every highlight in the whole painting will refer back to this decision.

**Viewpoint — deciding the angle you are painting from:**
Think about photographs of a building. One was taken from street level looking up — the building looks tall and imposing. Another was taken from above — the building looks like a flat map. A third was taken from the side at eye level — the building looks ordinary and familiar. Each photograph shows the same building, but the angle creates a completely different feeling. Your viewpoint decision works the same way: write down whether you are looking up at the subject, down at it, straight across at it, or from some unusual angle. This is cheapest to decide now, because changing the angle after the detailed drawing is started means starting the structural drawing over.

**Canvas setup — setting the size and technical settings before you paint:**
Think of buying a picture frame before you paint the picture. If you choose a tiny frame and then decide you want a much larger painting, you cannot simply stretch the frame — you have to start again. A digital canvas works the same way. Set it to the size and technical settings the finished image needs at the very beginning. If you want to post it online, that needs one set of settings. If you might ever print it, it needs a larger size and different settings. Making the canvas too small and then enlarging it later blurs all the fine detail you painted, the same way enlarging a small photo makes it look soft and blocky.

**Ideation — trying out a few different directions before committing to one:**
Think of a chef deciding what to cook for a dinner party. Before shopping or chopping anything, they think through three possible menus: pasta, roast, or fish. Takes two minutes. They compare the options, pick the one that suits the occasion, and only then go shopping. Ideation is that two-minute comparison step for your painting: before drawing or painting anything, write a short description of two or three different ways the image could go — different arrangements, different moods, different approaches — and compare them against your brief. The one that best fits your written goal is the one you develop. Changing your mind at this stage costs nothing. Changing your mind after four hours of detailed painting costs four hours.

### What to actually do in your software

1. **Before opening the painting app: write your brief in a notes app, a text file, or on paper.** Answer these questions in plain sentences: What do I want this image to show? What mood or feeling should it have? Where will it be seen (screen, print, social media)? How realistic do I want it to look? Where is the light coming from? What angle am I looking from? Keep this note visible while you work.

2. **Write two or three rough alternative directions — just in words is fine.** For example: "Option A — the figure lit from behind so it is mostly silhouette, moody and dark. Option B — lit from the front, cheerful and clear, bright colours. Option C — lit from the side, dramatic, half in shadow." Read each one against your brief. Pick the one that fits. Write one sentence explaining why — that sentence will save you doubt later.

3. **Open your painting app and create a new canvas at the correct size.** If the image is for social media or screen only: start at 3000 pixels on the long side and use sRGB colour mode. If you think you might ever print it: start at 2480 × 3508 pixels (A4) or larger, use the widest colour mode your app offers, and work in 16-bit if available. In Procreate, these are set when you tap the + to create a new canvas. In Photoshop or Krita, they appear in the New Document dialog.

4. **Save the file immediately with a clear name.** Use a name that includes the date and a short description — for example `dragon-forest-2026-10-01-v01`. Save a copy of your brief text into the same folder. You now have a record of the goal and a correctly set-up canvas, and Stage 01 is done.

### Common traps to avoid

**Starting to paint without writing anything down.** The brief does not have to be long — three sentences is enough. But without writing it, you are making every future decision from memory of a vague idea. When the painting starts to feel directionless two hours in, the brief is what you return to. If it was never written, there is nothing to return to.

**Only thinking of one idea and going straight to it.** The first idea that comes to mind is not always the best one. Taking two minutes to describe two alternatives — even just in words — almost always surfaces a better option or reveals a weakness in the first idea before you have invested any time in it.

**Picking the canvas size based on what is already open or what feels easy.** Small canvases are fast to work on, but if you ever decide to print the image or use it at a larger size, you cannot get the detail back. Start at the size the finished image actually needs.

**Deciding the style is "realistic" without thinking through what that requires.** Photorealistic painting requires careful attention to how every surface catches the light, very smooth blending, and precise proportions. If that is more work than you want to do, a stylized or graphic approach may be a better fit — and that decision shapes which references you collect, how you draw the construction, and how detailed the rendering needs to be. There is no wrong answer, but the decision needs to be made now, not discovered halfway through Stage 08.
