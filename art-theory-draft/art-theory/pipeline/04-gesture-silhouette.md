# Stage 04 — Gesture & Silhouette

**Status:** Work in progress.

Stage 04 establishes the primary directional energy of the pose or form, and confirms that the outer shape communicates clearly before any structural detail is added. The governing questions are: *Does the pose or major form read clearly at a distance?* and *Can the subject be identified from its outline alone?* Gesture and silhouette problems not resolved here become progressively harder and more expensive to fix once construction, rendering, and detail are layered on top.

---

## What You Do at This Stage

- On a separate layer, draw the primary action line — a single sweeping mark that captures the main direction and energy of the pose or form before any individual part is drawn.
- Rough in the major masses as large, simplified blobs following that action line. Do not draw individual features, fingers, or anatomical detail at this stage.
- Check the silhouette: fill the main subject with flat black on a new layer. Look only at the outer shape. Can you identify what the subject is from the black cutout alone?
- Look for stiffness: straight lines running parallel through the body produce a rigid, static pose. Introduce counter-curves (the spine curves one way; the hips tip the other) to create dynamic opposition.
- Confirm the gesture still reads from the thumbnail approved in Stage 03. If the gesture was established there, preserve it here.

---

## Stage Sign-off Condition

**You can move on when:** The flat-black silhouette of the main subject communicates what it is and what it is doing, and the primary directional energy is visible as a single readable flow.

---

## Common Failure Modes

| Symptom | What is happening | How to address it |
|---|---|---|
| Subject unidentifiable as a silhouette | Key parts are merging — arm blends into body, weapon blends into background | Separate merged parts by changing their angle; introduce a gap or clear overlap |
| Pose reads as stiff or static | Major joints form straight parallel lines | Tilt the hips opposite the shoulders; introduce a C or S curve through the major axis |
| Gesture visible in thumbnail has drifted | Scaling up from thumbnail drifted the arrangement | Overlay the approved thumbnail semi-transparently; redraw to match its major masses and action line |
| Details drawn before gesture is locked | Anatomy or fabric folds being established before outer silhouette reads clearly | Hide the detail layer; resolve the gesture and silhouette check first |
| Subject floats — no ground contact | Centre of mass is unsupported | Locate the centre of mass; ensure support structure (foot, base) is directly below it |

## Plain-English Guide

> **Who this is for:** Someone opening a digital painting app for the first time with no formal art training.

### What is the Gesture & Silhouette stage?

Gesture and silhouette are the two most important decisions you make about any figure or object before drawing a single anatomical detail. Gesture is the main direction and energy of the pose — think of it as the big sweep of a body from head to toes, captured in one flowing line. Silhouette is the outer edge of that form: the shape you would see if you cut the subject out of black paper. Both must be clear and readable before you draw anything inside them. A beautifully rendered face on a stiff, blobby body does not fix the body — it just draws attention to it. Getting gesture and silhouette right early is cheap. Fixing them after the rendering is done is brutal.

### The key ideas, without the jargon

**The spine of the pose (action line — that means the single most important directional flow through the whole body or form):** Think of a toy bow being drawn back. The bow bends in one direction, under tension, with energy ready to release. A dynamic pose has the same quality — the spine, leg, tail, or whatever the main axis is has a clear bend or lean, like a spring under load. A pose where everything runs straight up like a lamppost has no tension in it at all. Before drawing anything detailed, draw one flowing line that captures the main direction of the whole subject from top to bottom — that is your action line.

**The outer shape as the first story (silhouette — that means the overall outline of your subject with all internal detail removed):** Think of the Batman logo — just the black bat shape on a yellow background. Cover the yellow and look only at the black shape. You can immediately tell it is a bat, because the outer shape is designed to communicate that. Now imagine a character whose sword arm sits at the exact same angle as their body — they merge into one indistinct blob. A good silhouette tells you what the subject is and roughly what it is doing before any detail is visible.

**Curves working against each other (counter-curves — that means bending the body in two opposite directions at once, which creates a feeling of movement):** Hold your hand flat and horizontal. Now tilt your wrist slightly and angle your elbow the opposite way. The slight opposing tilts immediately look more natural and dynamic than everything in one flat line. A figure works the same way: the hips tilt slightly one way, the shoulders tilt the other way, and the spine makes a gentle S between them. That opposition is what makes a pose look living rather than wooden.

**The merged blob problem (tangent — that means two shapes accidentally touching or lining up so they cannot be read separately):** Imagine a silhouette photo where someone stands in front of a dark tree and their head merges exactly with the trunk into one big vertical blob. The same happens when a character's arm runs parallel to their body, or two figures at the same brightness stand side by side. The fix is always the same: change the angle or brightness of one thing so a visible separation appears between them.

### What to actually do in your software

1. **Create a new layer called "Gesture."** With a large, low-opacity brush, draw one single flowing line from the head (or top) to the feet (or base) capturing the main direction and lean of your subject. This is a single mark, not a detailed drawing — it is a guide only.

2. **On the same or a second rough layer, block in the main masses as large flat blobs.** Head is a blob. Ribcage is a blob. Hips are a blob. No fingers, facial features, or clothing detail. Fill these roughly following the action line — the top blob leans slightly one way, the hip blob leans the other.

3. **Silhouette check: create a new layer above everything.** Fill the main subject with solid black. Now look only at the outer edge. Zoom out to 25%. Can you tell what it is? Can you see what it is doing? If it is a blob with unclear limbs, separate the parts before proceeding.

4. **Counter-curve check: look at your gesture line.** Is any section of the body a completely straight vertical or horizontal line? Introduce a slight opposing tilt — tip the hips slightly left, the shoulders slightly right, or vice versa.

### Common traps to avoid

- **Drawing details before the silhouette is sorted.** Until the outer black cutout reads clearly, all internal detail is built on an unstable foundation. Get the silhouette right first.

- **Making the pose too symmetrical.** When both arms hang at exactly the same angle and both legs point straight down, the figure looks like it is standing at attention. Real bodies almost never align perfectly — introduce one asymmetry and the pose immediately reads as more natural.

- **Ignoring the action line in complex subjects.** With vehicles, creatures, or architecture, there is still a dominant directional flow — the angle of a spaceship nose, the arc of a dragon's spine. Find and draw that line first.

- **Forgetting that silhouette problems grow with rendering.** A character whose arm merges with their body becomes increasingly hard to separate as rendering adds more detail to the same-brightness area. Separate merged silhouette elements now.
